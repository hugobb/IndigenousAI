# Decolonizing the Digital Landscape: The Role of Technology in Indigenous Language Revitalization

**Authors:** Paul J. Meighan
**Year:** 2021
**Venue:** AlterNative: An International Journal of Indigenous Peoples, Vol. 17(3)

---

## Core Argument

Technology is not neutral: it extends the knowledge and belief systems that created it, and the World Wide Web was built by and for a Western, English-speaking audience. Over three decades since the Web's founding, Indigenous communities have nonetheless used digital technologies to advance language revitalization — contributing to a gradual decolonization of the digital landscape. The paper synthesizes this history and proposes a taxonomy of technology types and stages to frame future ILR technology work.

## Key Concepts

- **Technology is not neutral:** "Technology is the extension of the knowledge and belief system which has led to its creation" (Strate, 2012). The implication: any technology used for Indigenous language revitalization carries the epistemological assumptions of its creators and must be critically evaluated.
- **Six technology types:** Facilitation → Communication → Web 1.0 (~1990–2005) → Web 2.0 (~2005–2015) → Web 3.0 (~2015–present) → Semantic technologies (AI/AR/VR, future). Each stage reflects a different relationship between technology creators and users, from top-down broadcast to peer-to-peer to AI-mediated interaction.
- **Relational approach:** The most successful ILR technology initiatives are those that foster relationships — between community members, between generations, between speakers and learners — rather than those that optimize for individual language exposure metrics.
- **Decolonizing the digital landscape:** Indigenous communities have not merely adopted Western digital tools; they have adapted and repurposed them for ILR goals, challenging the monolingual English design of the Web and creating digital spaces where Indigenous languages are present and functional.
- **Whose knowledge system?** The central decolonial question for any ILR technology: who created it, for what purpose, how is data shared and stored, and whose epistemological framework governs design decisions?

## Main Findings

- From the earliest Web 1.0 era, Indigenous communities pioneered ILR technology use: Te Wahapu (Māori, 1990) and Leoki ("Powerful Voice," Hawaiian, 1993) predate most mainstream digital language tools.
- Each technological generation (Web 1.0 through 3.0) has enabled new forms of ILR engagement: documentation and archiving (1.0), social media community-building (2.0), mobile and peer-to-peer production (3.0).
- The relationship between technology users and audiences has evolved from state/group broadcasting to mass audience (Web 1.0) to bidirectional peer-to-peer (Web 2.0) — a shift that has empowered Indigenous communities to produce and distribute their own language content.
- Semantic technologies (AI, 3D avatars, blockchain) represent the next frontier; their relationship to ILR is currently speculative but potentially transformative.
- The most impactful ILR technology projects are those grounded in community relationships, not those with the most sophisticated technology.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

Meighan's paper provides essential historical and critical framing for the IndigenousAI project. Three points are particularly relevant. First, the neutrality critique: any AI tool for Mohawk carries the assumptions of the models, training data, and design frameworks that created it — which are overwhelmingly non-Indigenous. This requires ongoing critical evaluation. Second, the relational technology argument: the IndigenousAI project should be evaluated not just on whether it teaches Mohawk vocabulary or grammar, but on whether it fosters relationships within the Six Nations community and between learners and Elders. Third, the historical continuity: Mohawk communities have been using digital technology for language revitalization for decades (the Kanien'kéha reference in Meighan's bibliography, Kawennón:nis verb conjugator); any new AI tool must connect to and build on this existing ecosystem rather than starting from scratch.

## Limitations & Critiques

- The paper is a conceptual review without primary data; it synthesizes existing literature through a decolonizing lens but does not report new empirical findings.
- The six technology types (facilitation through semantic) are Western categorizations applied retrospectively; Indigenous communities may not understand their technology use through this framework.
- Meighan is a Scottish Gael, not an Indigenous North American scholar; while his positionality is carefully articulated, the paper's generalizations about Indigenous experience should be read with this context in mind.
- Written in 2021, the paper predates large language models and the specific risks and opportunities they introduce for ILR; the "semantic technologies" section is necessarily speculative.

## Questions & Follow-ups

- What does a "relational approach" to AI-assisted language learning look like for Mohawk? How could an AI tool be designed to foster relationships rather than individual engagement?
- How have Kanien'kéha (Mohawk) communities used Web 2.0 and Web 3.0 technologies for language revitalization? What social media, YouTube, or app-based ILR initiatives already exist?
- Related work: Brinklow (2021) on anti-colonial ILR and digital sovereignty; Herrera (2022) on identity and technology in revitalization; Godwin-Jones (2025) on AI pedagogical tools and place-language connection; Ajani et al. (2024) on ethical consent.
