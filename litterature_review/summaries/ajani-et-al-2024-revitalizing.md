# Revitalizing Indigenous Knowledge Systems via Digital Media Technologies for Sustainability of Indigenous Languages

**Authors:** Yusuf Ayodeji Ajani, Bolaji David Oladokun, Shuaib Agboola Olarongbe, Margaret Nkechi Amaechi, Nafisa Rabiu, Musediq Tunji Bashorun
**Year:** 2024
**Venue:** PDT&C — Preservation, Digital Technology & Culture, 53(1): 35–44 (De Gruyter)

---

## Core Argument

Digital media technologies are a critical lever for preserving and revitalizing Indigenous languages in the Fourth Industrial Revolution, but their effectiveness depends on community-led governance, collaborative partnerships, and attention to ethical considerations including intellectual property and informed consent. The paper argues that technology is a "force multiplier" for human revitalization work, not a substitute for it.

## Key Concepts

- **Fourth Industrial Revolution (4IR):** The current era of technological transformation, used here to contextualize the urgency of Indigenous language preservation in an increasingly digital communication environment.
- **Digital documentation and archiving:** Using audio/video recording, digital storage, and digitization to capture oral traditions, songs, manuscripts, and other linguistic resources for long-term preservation.
- **Language learning applications:** Mobile apps, online platforms, and interactive software offering self-paced vocabulary acquisition, grammar instruction, gamification, and multimedia elements for endangered language learners.
- **Digital divide:** Disparities in access to technology and internet connectivity that may exclude remote Indigenous communities from benefiting equally from digital preservation tools.
- **Community-led preservation:** The principle that Indigenous communities must actively participate in defining goals, shaping initiatives, and maintaining sovereignty over their cultural data in digital projects.

## Main Findings

- Digital media technology enables documentation, archiving, digitization, and dissemination of Indigenous languages at scale, reaching learners beyond the geographic footprint of language communities.
- Social media platforms (Facebook, YouTube, Instagram), mobile apps, and VR/AR create new spaces for language promotion, community networking, and immersive learning experiences.
- Case studies from Africa (South Africa's Indigenous Digital Archive, Ghana's Sankofa Digital Archive, Tanzania's "Ntongato" app, Nigeria's "Learn Yoruba" app) illustrate concrete implementations.
- Collaboration between indigenous communities, linguists, academia, tech companies, and libraries is identified as essential; no single actor can sustain preservation alone.
- Long-term sustainability requires dedicated funding, community ownership, intergenerational transmission programs, continuous technological adaptation, and supportive government policy.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

This review provides a broad orientation to the digital language revitalization ecosystem — the landscape of tools and community practices within which an AI-assisted Mohawk language tool would need to situate itself. The recurring emphasis on community agency, ethical consent, and intellectual property directly informs the IndigenousAI project's approach: any tool must be co-designed with Mohawk community members and governed under community-defined protocols. The paper also highlights the digital divide risk: Mohawk language learners in remote Six Nations contexts may have limited internet access, which constrains the deployment profile of cloud-based AI tools.

## Limitations & Critiques

- The paper is a literature review with no primary data; findings are synthesized from secondary sources of variable quality and mostly concern African and non-North American contexts.
- Generative AI and LLMs are not addressed; the paper predates widespread deployment of large language models for endangered language work.
- Community consent and intellectual property are mentioned as important but not analyzed in depth; no operational frameworks are provided.

## Questions & Follow-ups

- What governance frameworks exist for intellectual property in Indigenous digital archives, and which are most transferable to a Mohawk AI project?
- How have the digital divide constraints changed since 2024, particularly for First Nations reserve communities in Ontario?
- Related work: Brinklow (2021) (digital colonization and Indigenous language technology), Brinklow et al. (2019) (language reclamation principles in Canada), Meighan (2021) (relational technology for Indigenous language revitalization).
