# Generative AI, Pedagogical Translanguaging, and Less Commonly Taught Languages

**Authors:** Robert Godwin-Jones
**Year:** 2025
**Venue:** Language Learning & Technology (or similar applied linguistics journal)

---

## Core Argument

Generative AI presents both opportunities and risks for the pedagogy of less commonly taught languages (LCTLs), including Indigenous languages. The framework of "pedagogical translanguaging" — deliberately drawing on learners' full linguistic repertoire across languages — offers a principled way to integrate AI tools without undermining the community-grounded, immersive approaches that are most effective for Indigenous language revitalization. The paper argues that the place-language connection unique to Indigenous languages demands pedagogical approaches that AI tools, by their globally placeless nature, are structurally ill-suited to support.

## Key Concepts

- **LCTLs (Less Commonly Taught Languages):** Languages outside the dominant European and Asian languages that receive relatively little pedagogical research or technological support; Indigenous languages are an extreme case.
- **Pedagogical translanguaging:** A pedagogical framework that treats multilingual learners' full repertoire as a resource rather than an interference; allows strategic use of L1 and other languages in L2 learning contexts. Contrasts with strict immersion approaches that forbid any language other than the target.
- **Place-language connection:** For many Indigenous languages, language is ontologically tied to land — words, place names, and speech acts are embedded in specific territorial and ecological contexts. This connection is a form of knowledge that cannot be replicated in decontextualized digital environments.
- **Immersion tension:** The most effective Indigenous language programs (Hawaiian, Māori) use total immersion — no English, no code-switching. Generative AI tools are inherently multilingual and operate outside the community context, creating structural tension with immersion pedagogy.
- **Generative AI affordances:** AI can provide on-demand practice, personalized feedback, and 24/7 availability — valuable for LCTLs with few human teachers. But AI outputs for LCTLs are unreliable, culturally thin, and may normalize incorrect forms.

## Main Findings

- Generative AI performs substantially worse on LCTLs than on dominant languages, both in grammatical accuracy and cultural appropriateness; for Indigenous languages with minimal training data, outputs are often unreliable.
- Pedagogical translanguaging provides a theoretical framework for integrating AI tools in ways that support rather than undermine Indigenous language learning — by positioning the AI as a supplementary resource rather than a primary language model.
- The place-language connection that characterizes many Indigenous languages is a pedagogical resource that cannot be replicated by AI; community-based immersion and land-based learning remain irreplaceable.
- The tension between immersion pedagogy (which prohibits code-switching) and AI tools (which are inherently multilingual) is real and requires deliberate pedagogical design to navigate.
- Community members and Elders remain the essential pedagogical resource for Indigenous languages; AI can extend reach and provide practice opportunities but cannot substitute for human community-grounded transmission.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

The IndigenousAI project should take seriously Godwin-Jones's caution about AI as a "MacGuffin" in language pedagogy (resonating with Brinklow et al.'s critique). For Mohawk/Six Nations, the place-language dimension is especially salient: Kanien'kéha (Mohawk) is tied to the territory of the Haudenosaunee, and language learning separated from that territorial context may produce a linguistically competent but culturally shallow learner. The pedagogical translanguaging framework is useful for the specific use case of AI-assisted Mohawk learning: position AI tools as supplementary practice for learners who are already engaged in community-based immersion, not as a replacement for it. The finding that AI outputs are unreliable for LCTLs reinforces that any Mohawk AI tool must be validated by fluent speakers before deployment.

## Limitations & Critiques

- The paper reviews pedagogical approaches primarily in higher education LCTL contexts; the community-based revitalization context (where there may be no classroom at all) is different in important ways.
- "Pedagogical translanguaging" is a framework developed in multilingual education contexts; its applicability to Indigenous language immersion programs (which often explicitly reject translanguaging) requires more nuanced discussion.
- The paper does not present empirical data on AI use in LCTL classrooms; it is a conceptual synthesis rather than an empirical study.

## Questions & Follow-ups

- What specific AI tools have been tested in LCTL classrooms and with what outcomes? Are there any Indigenous language cases?
- How do Hawaiian or Māori immersion programs approach digital and AI tools — do they integrate them or exclude them from immersion environments?
- Related work: Brinklow et al. (2019) on technology as MacGuffin; Brinklow (2021) on anti-colonial ILT; Meighan (2021) on relational technology; Ajani et al. (2024) on community agency.
