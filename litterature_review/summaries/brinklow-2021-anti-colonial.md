# Indigenous Language Technologies: Anti-Colonial Oases in a Colonizing (Digital) World

**Authors:** Nathan Thanyehténhas Brinklow
**Year:** 2021
**Venue:** WINHEC: International Journal of Indigenous Education Scholarship, 16(1), pp. 239–266 (Special Issue on Indigenous Language Revitalization)

---

## Core Argument

The global technology ecosystem constitutes an active form of digital colonization: large multinational corporations (Google, Apple, Amazon, Microsoft) build language technology for a small handful of majority languages, imposing linguistic hierarchies that marginalize Indigenous communities. Indigenous leadership in language technology development is not merely beneficial but politically necessary — a form of anticolonial resistance that creates protected digital spaces ("oases") where Indigenous languages can flourish on community-defined terms.

## Key Concepts

- **Digital colonization:** The process by which technology companies impose majority-language infrastructure on digital communication environments, structurally disadvantaging Indigenous languages and undermining their vitality.
- **Indigenous language technologies (ILT):** Tools and systems — keyboards, spell-checkers, speech recognition, predictive text, translation — designed for and, ideally, by Indigenous language communities.
- **Responsive and responsible ILT:** Brinklow's standard for good Indigenous language technology: responsive to community-identified needs; responsible in its governance, data practices, and respect for cultural protocols.
- **Indigenous leadership:** Defined broadly as Indigenous people and language speakers involved in all aspects of technology development — identifying problems, finding solutions, exercising control, and deploying tools back to their communities.
- **Anti-colonial oases:** Digital spaces and tools where Indigenous languages are centered rather than marginalized — spaces that actively push back against the dominant technological and linguistic order.
- **Speech technology:** A specific case study in the paper (Siri-style voice assistants, ASR); the near-total absence of Indigenous language support in commercial speech technology is analyzed as a structural effect of digital colonization.

## Main Findings

- The global language technology ecosystem is dominated by a handful of companies supporting ~5–10 languages; 96% of the world's languages — including all Indigenous North American languages — are excluded.
- Indigenous language revitalization goals are broader than linguistic competence: they are connected to identity, community building, cultural resurgence, and self-determination. Technology that serves only the linguistic goal while undermining community sovereignty fails on the project's own terms.
- Indigenous leadership in ILT development challenges dominant assumptions about what technology is for, who it serves, and what values it encodes — and creates technologies better aligned with community needs.
- Despite hostile conditions (data scarcity, funding gaps, corporate monopolies), Indigenous-led projects have produced effective tools: keyboards, spell-checkers, corpus builders, and voice data collection frameworks.
- The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the digital transition of community life, raising the stakes for Indigenous language presence in digital spaces.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

Brinklow writes from a Mohawk (Kanien'kéha) community standpoint (Queen's University / Tyendinaga Mohawk Territory), making this the most directly community-grounded paper in the corpus for the IndigenousAI project's specific focus. The concept of "responsive and responsible ILT" directly defines the standard the IndigenousAI project should hold itself to. The analysis of digital colonization provides the political context within which AI-for-Indigenous-languages work must be understood — particularly the risk that well-intentioned AI tools reproduce colonial dynamics if community leadership is not structurally embedded. The anti-colonial oases framing reframes the project's goal: not merely to build a useful tool, but to build a tool that actively serves Mohawk community sovereignty.

## Limitations & Critiques

- The paper is primarily analytical/theoretical and does not report empirical results from specific ILT deployments; its strength is framing rather than evidence.
- Written in 2021, the paper predates the current generation of large language models and cannot address the specific opportunities and risks that LLMs pose for Indigenous language work.
- "Indigenous leadership" is defined in terms of participation and control but does not operationalize what meaningful community governance looks like in practice for AI systems.

## Questions & Follow-ups

- What would an Indigenous-led AI development process look like specifically for Mohawk language tools — who are the decision-makers, what protocols govern data use, and how are outputs validated by the community?
- How has the Tyendinaga Mohawk community specifically engaged with language technology development, and what lessons transfer to the IndigenousAI project?
- Related work: Brinklow et al. (2019) (Indigenous language technologies and reclamation principles in Canada), Meighan (2021) (relational technology for Indigenous language revitalization), Pinhanez & Wornyo (2025) (co-development frameworks for Indigenous AI).
