# Literature Review — Synthesis

> Last updated: 2026-05-28. 92 papers reviewed.

## Scope

This review synthesizes 92 papers relevant to the development of generative AI with and for Indigenous communities, with a current focus on the Mohawk language (Kanien'kéha) and the Six Nations community. Papers were selected for their bearing on six intersecting concerns: epistemic justice and representational harms produced by large language models; NLP tools and evaluation frameworks for low-resource, endangered, and extremely low-resource (XLR) languages — including polysynthetic and oral-tradition languages; ethical and decolonial methodologies for community co-construction; evaluation and benchmarking of language models on culturally specific and linguistically novel tasks; prompting and in-context learning strategies for XLR translation; and technical infrastructure for language-community AI systems — including tokenization, morphological NLP, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), knowledge graphs, and low-resource LLM adaptation. The review has grown substantially in its coverage of: the Canadian Indigenous language technology ecosystem (NRC Canada, OCAP® principles, Algonquian and Iroquoian NLP); tokenization and morphological segmentation for polysynthetic languages; RAG and GraphRAG architectures and their evaluation; low-resource LLM fine-tuning and continued pre-training; and community ethics frameworks for AI co-development. The review serves the IndigenousAI project at Mila (Montréal Institute of Learning Algorithms), which seeks to develop AI tools that support Mohawk language revitalization in genuine partnership with the Six Nations community.

## Thematic Synthesis

### Epistemic Justice & Representation

The literature reviewed here converged on a central finding: large language models encode and reproduce epistemic hierarchies that systematically disadvantage non-Western, non-English, and especially Indigenous communities. Kay et al. (2024) offered the most theoretically developed account of this dynamic, introducing the concept of "generative algorithmic epistemic injustice" — a distinct form of harm that goes beyond classification-based bias. Their taxonomy identified four modes: amplified testimonial injustice (models propagate social prejudices at mass scale), manipulative testimonial injustice (deliberate AI-assisted fabrication targeting marginalized groups), hermeneutical ignorance (models lack the embodied cultural depth to represent certain experiences faithfully), and hermeneutical access injustice (qualitatively inferior model outputs for speakers of under-resourced languages). The last two modes are structurally embedded in current architectures — they are not incidental failures but consequences of how training data is assembled and how alignment is performed.

Barman et al. (2025) traced one of the primary mechanisms of this harm to the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipeline. The initial evaluator pool for models such as InstructGPT was drawn from a narrow demographic (predominantly young, university-educated, Southeast Asian and American workers on crowdsourcing platforms), with no Indigenous evaluators represented in disclosed demographics. Drawing on social epistemology, they argued that this structural exclusion produces sycophancy — models learn to please the evaluators who trained them, amplifying those evaluators' cultural assumptions — and that remediation requires not only diversifying evaluators but reorganizing feedback aggregation methods so that minority viewpoints are preserved rather than averaged out. Their analysis of positionality directly supported the epistemic claim that Indigenous community members possess a form of knowledge about AI systems affecting their communities that outside researchers cannot substitute for.

Adilazuarda et al. (2024) surveyed more than ninety studies of culture in LLMs and found that none explicitly defined culture; all relied on proxies — most commonly country of origin or language — that rendered Indigenous nations, whose cultural territories do not align with state borders, effectively invisible. Their distinction between "thin" and "thick" description named a further dimension of the representational deficit: LLMs tend to produce outsider-view, thin accounts of cultural practices, lacking the interpretive context that community members themselves supply. Pawar et al. (2025) and Liu et al. (2025), surveying the broader landscape of culturally aware NLP, independently confirmed the skew: Africa, Central Asia, South/Southeast Asia, and Indigenous cultures worldwide were severely underrepresented in both training data and evaluation benchmarks, while Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) cultures dominated. Singh et al. (2025) made the WEIRD bias concrete and measurable: 86.5% of the culturally sensitive questions in the MMLU benchmark reflected Western culture, and 64.5% required North American regional knowledge specifically.

Khanuja et al. (2026) examined the representational deficit from a mechanistic angle. Using sparse autoencoders to probe internal model representations, they showed that LLMs encode culture-specific knowledge in linearly decodable internal features and that under implicit prompting, 60% of Gemma-2-9B's cultural defaults aligned with the United States or United Kingdom. For cultures with sparse training-data presence — the situation of any Indigenous language community — the model's latent cultural knowledge was either absent or unelicitable by standard prompting, even when some representation existed. This finding suggested that representational failure for Indigenous communities is twofold: a data absence problem and an elicitation failure problem, with different remedies for each. The technical papers on extremely low-resource language translation added a further dimension: Court and Elsner (2024) noted that LLM-based methods risk overstating feasibility for communities, raising expectations about AI assistance that cannot realistically be met, a form of epistemic harm distinct from misrepresentation but equally consequential for trust. Hutson et al. (2024) reinforced the structural dimension: AI systems are accelerating the dominance of a small number of "data-rich" languages, creating new forms of linguistic colonization even within ostensibly multilingual AI systems.

### Language Revitalization & NLP

The reviewed literature on low-resource and endangered Indigenous languages grew substantially with the addition of twenty-two papers focused on machine translation, in-context learning, dictionary-augmented prompting, structured textbook prompting, transliteration strategies, linguistically diverse prompting, grammar-book translation, and linguistic reasoning for extremely low-resource (XLR) languages. Taken together, these papers trace a coherent emerging paradigm: in the absence of parallel corpora, existing linguistic documentation — grammar books, dictionaries, morphological analyzers — can be leveraged as in-context resources for LLMs, and the resulting system outputs can be used to bootstrap conventional translation models at a fraction of the cost of full fine-tuning.

Bird (2020) provided the foundational critical analysis, arguing that the speech and language technology field had reproduced colonial logics — treating Indigenous languages as data to be extracted, problems to be solved — and had accumulated little evidence that technology had meaningfully reversed language shift. His critique targeted the "gold data ideology" (preoccupation with clean, annotated corpora) and the "zero-resource" framing (which erased existing community knowledge). Zhang et al. (2022), working with the Cherokee language, operationalized an alternative into a three-stage roadmap — community relationship building first, NLP-assisted education second, language-specific NLP research third — and found that community members had expressed specific technology priorities (machine translation for drafting and editing, OCR for digitizing manuscripts, ASR for audio transcription) that differed from what the NLP community had been producing. Liu et al. (2022), working with four Indigenous communities including Cayuga (a closely related Haudenosaunee language), extended the ethical dimension: they distinguished endangered languages from other low-resource languages on the grounds that the transcription bottleneck, the irreplaceability of Master Speakers (fluent elders who have accepted apprentices through oral tradition), and the historical trauma of linguicide created constraints that standard NLP assumptions violated.

The AmericasNLP shared tasks documented the state of the art for machine translation into Indigenous languages of the Americas. Ebrahimi et al. (2023) reported that despite average ChrF improvements of nearly ten points since 2021, human evaluation showed near-perfect translations remained extremely rare and that Otomí outputs were rated 1 out of 5 on both fluency and meaning for the majority of evaluated sentences. De Gibert et al. (2025) observed a performance plateau: the NLLB-1.3B multilingual fine-tuned baseline was extremely difficult to surpass, and rule-based and knowledge-grounded approaches consistently outperformed pure LLM prompting for morphologically complex, low-resource languages. Nahuatl (Western Sierra Puebla variety) achieved only 17.5% exact-match accuracy under the best submitted system. Mager et al. (2023) provided a comprehensive NLP survey for Indigenous languages of the Americas, documenting specific challenges under extremely low-resource (ELR) conditions: data scarcity across all modalities, typological divergence from high-resource languages, lack of standardized orthographies, and absence of speaker communities in the research process. Tonja et al. (2024) extended this picture globally: of 650 indigenous Latin American languages, only ~22 of Mexico's 68 officially recognized languages appear in any NLP research, confirming that coverage gaps are even more severe than aggregate statistics suggest. Haddow et al. (2022), surveying the broader landscape of low-resource MT, provided the essential technical foundation: no single approach dominates across data regimes, transfer from related languages is most effective when source and target are typologically similar, and tokenization and vocabulary design matter significantly for morphologically complex languages where subword methods can fail.

Against this backdrop, the grammar-book paradigm emerged as the most practically applicable approach for XLR languages. Tanzer et al. (2024) demonstrated the proof-of-concept: a single grammar book provided as in-context material to a long-context LLM enabled meaningful translation of Kalamang (a Papuan language effectively unseen by current LLMs), substantially exceeding zero-shot baselines. The combination of vocabulary entries and parallel sentence examples (the W+S condition) produced the best overall quality; adding explicit grammatical descriptions sometimes helped with specific constructions but also introduced distractions. Aycock et al. (2025) subsequently interrogated the mechanism behind these gains: almost all translation improvement from grammar-book prompting derived from the parallel sentence examples embedded in the book, not from grammatical explanations per se. They further demonstrated that a fine-tuned encoder-decoder model trained on those same parallel examples could match or exceed grammar-book LLM performance. This was confirmed from a different angle by Court and Elsner (2024), studying Southern Quechua: morpheme and word-level translations reliably improved LLM outputs, while grammar descriptions had null or negative effects regardless of model size, and improvements with model size primarily reflected prior language exposure in pretraining rather than better in-context learning.

Pei et al. (2025) extended this line of inquiry to Manchu, using an encipherment control to eliminate any residual LLM prior knowledge of the language and thereby isolating the true contribution of in-context resources. High-quality dictionaries and retrieved parallel examples significantly improved translation quality, while grammar books contributed little. Crucially, Pei et al. (2025) also demonstrated that ICL-generated synthetic parallel data could bootstrap a conventional MT model that was more efficient and often outperformed the ICL approach alone. Zhang et al. (2024, LINGOLLM) showed that combining morphological analyzers, dictionaries, and grammar books into a four-step pipeline raised GPT-4's average translation BLEU from 0.5 to 10.5 across ten translation directions for eight endangered languages including Manchu, Gitksan, Arapaho, and Bribri. Morphological analysis added approximately 19% BLEURT improvement over dictionary-only lookup, and humans using the same resources still outperformed the system (BLEU 20.32 vs. 9.12 on Manchu).

The dictionary-augmented paradigm was substantially extended by two new papers. Lu et al. (2024) proposed Chain-of-Dictionary (CoD) prompting, which augments the LLM with chained multilingual dictionary entries — expressing each word's meaning across multiple intermediate languages rather than just the source-target pair. CoD achieved gains of up to 13× chrF++ over zero-shot baselines and outperformed few-shot ICL for low-resource translation, demonstrating that multiple alignment signals from a dictionary chain compensated for the absence of parallel examples. Zhang et al. (2024, DIPMTT++) operationalized a complementary framework for languages entirely unseen by LLMs, combining dictionary lookup, parallel sentence retrieval, synonym expansion, and fuzzy matching. Using only a dictionary and 5K parallel sentences, DIPMTT++ enabled GPT-4 to achieve 16 BLEU on Chinese-to-Zhuang translation (from a baseline of 0) and 32 BLEU in the reverse direction. Taken together with Pei et al. (2025) and Zhang et al. (2024, LINGOLLM), these results established dictionaries as the single most reliable in-context resource for XLR translation — consistently more effective than grammar books when provided in isolation.

Guo et al. (2024), with the TALENT framework, proposed a structured alternative to unstructured grammar-book prompting: TALENT constructs a structured textbook for the target language and prompts the LLM to extract and internalize syntax patterns before attempting translation. Evaluated on 112 low-resource language directions from FLORES-200, TALENT achieved a consistent 14.8% improvement over zero-shot baselines. Zhang et al. (2025) further refined this picture by identifying rule retrieval as the dominant bottleneck: decomposing translation into explicit rule retrieval and rule application steps, and formatting grammar rules as pseudo-code functions rather than natural language prose, achieved a 13.1% BLEU gain over end-to-end grammar-book prompting for Zhuang.

The in-context learning literature expanded substantially beyond the grammar-book paradigm. Cahyawijaya et al. (2025) demonstrated that query alignment consistently outperformed the commonly used label alignment strategy for low-resource ICL across 25 low-resource languages. Li et al. (2025) established practitioner guidelines for the choice between ICL and fine-tuning: for XLR languages with unseen or rare scripts, zero-shot ICL with language alignment outperformed both few-shot ICL and PEFT — because PEFT updated model weights without adding new vocabulary coverage, degrading performance on languages whose scripts were fragmented by existing tokenizers. Ma et al. (2025) addressed a related tokenization challenge through transliteration: converting non-Latin script text to Latin script before in-context presentation improved NER performance by up to 25% F1 across multiple models. Nguyen et al. (2024) proposed Linguistically-Diverse Prompting (LDP), an unsupervised method that assembled synthetic few-shot exemplars from multiple high-resource languages without any labeled target-language data, performing on par with supervised few-shot learning for English-to-LRL translation across 34 languages. Zebaze et al. (2025) showed that similarity-based retrieval of in-context examples consistently outperformed random selection for low-resource language directions.

Yahan and Islam (2025) contributed one of the few papers to directly target Indigenous language education applications. Using LoRA and quantization-based fine-tuning of Llama 3.2 (3B), they achieved competitive sentence transformation results for Bribri, Guarani, and Maya. Song et al. (2026) established that knowledge distillation from a strong teacher model using predominantly monolingual low-resource data enabled SLMs to match or surpass systems up to 70B parameters on low-resource translation. Coleman et al. (2026) presented the first systematic comparison of five LLM-based translation approaches for Owens Valley Paiute (OVP), a critically endangered Indigenous language with no public parallel corpora; their hybrid LLM–Rule-Based MT pipeline guaranteed grammatical correctness while maintaining flexibility. Zhu et al. (2025) showed that step-by-step scaffolding substantially improved LLM linguistic reasoning performance, but all models systematically underperformed on languages with morphological structures deviating from patterns dominant in training data — a typological bias directly relevant to Mohawk's polysynthetic verb morphology.

### Tokenization, Morphology & Polysynthetic NLP

A newly consolidated cluster of papers addresses the technical challenges of tokenization and morphological NLP for languages that fall outside the typological mainstream assumed by standard NLP pipelines. This cluster is directly relevant to Mohawk, whose polysynthetic morphology produces extremely long words, productive verb forms that encode clause-level information, and a vocabulary that standard BPE tokenizers cannot efficiently represent.

**What drives tokenization performance for polysynthetic languages?** Four papers directly address this question. Goldman et al. (2024) established that BPE compression ratio — the average number of tokens per byte — is a reliable intrinsic predictor of downstream LM performance across languages, with a monotonic relationship: better compression correlates with better model performance. Vasques et al. (2023) provided a mechanistic partial explanation: BPE subword productivity (the proportion of productive, morpheme-like subwords) reflects morphological typology, with polysynthetic languages (exemplified by Late Eastern Inuit) occupying an extreme position in the subword productivity space. Schmidt et al. (2024), however, challenged the naive reading: a tokenizer (PathPiece) specifically designed to minimize token count consistently underperforms BPE despite achieving higher compression, demonstrating that compression is not the only relevant factor — pre-tokenization choices and BPE initialization also matter substantially. Poelman et al. (2025) added methodological caution: apparent relationships between morphology and LM performance are confounded by language set selection, coarse typological groupings, differing tokenization algorithms, and uncontrolled dataset sizes, making published claims about morphological complexity and model performance less reliable than they appear.

**Does morphological alignment of tokenizers improve performance?** Two papers by Arnett and colleagues directly address this question with negative results. Arnett and Bergen (2025) introduced MorphScore, a metric for token-morpheme boundary alignment across 22 languages, and found no significant correlation with LM perplexity — the morphological alignment hypothesis is not empirically supported. The performance gap between agglutinative and fusional languages is largely attributable to data disparity and the byte premium (extra bytes to encode morphologically rich languages), not to tokenizer design. Arnett et al. (2025) expanded MorphScore to 70 languages and replicated this null result. Alqahtani et al. (2026) provided the policy implications: tokenizer reuse (inheriting tokenizers trained for majority languages) is the most pervasive unexamined source of bias and inefficiency, and tokenizer design should be treated as a core decision co-designed with the model and deployment context.

**Morphological segmentation as an alternative to subword tokenization.** Where tokenization approaches BPE morphology implicitly, morphological segmentation approaches it explicitly. Kann et al. (2018) demonstrated that neural seq2seq morphological segmentation for polysynthetic languages could be trained with as little as 25% of the full training data using multi-task learning with auxiliary tasks, and that cross-lingual transfer from related languages provided substantial signal with minimal fine-tuning. Khandagale et al. (2022) showed that unsupervised Adaptor Grammar-based segmentation with linguistically motivated priors (stem maximization, paradigm completion) substantially outperformed fully unsupervised methods for Adyghe and Inuktitut, and enabled stem-level POS tagging despite no direct POS supervision. Liu et al. (2021) applied neural encoder-decoder segmentation specifically to Seneca — an Iroquoian language in the same family as Mohawk — achieving strong performance with fewer than 50 first-language speakers, and demonstrating that cross-linguistic transfer from Mohawk to Seneca (or vice versa) is linguistically justified given shared structural properties. Mager et al. (2022) compared BPE against morphological segmentation across four polysynthetic languages, finding that unsupervised morphological segmentation consistently outperformed BPE for extremely low-resource (ELR) translation, while supervised morphological segmentation unexpectedly underperformed in MT contexts — a finding suggesting that the relationship between segmentation quality and MT quality is not monotonic. Stenlund et al. (2025) demonstrated that LLM-based morphological segmentation (framing the problem as binary boundary classification) using Glot500-m on Inuktitut syllabics, with automatic annotation from the UQAILAUT FST as training data, outperforms or matches prior neural approaches — establishing an FST-bootstrapped LLM pipeline as a practical option for polysynthetic language segmentation.

**The NRC Canada ecosystem.** Several papers engage specifically with the Canadian institutional context for Indigenous language NLP. Kuhn et al. (2020) documented the NRC Canada Indigenous Language Technology (ILT) Phase I project: a $6M, 3-year effort producing Kawennón:nis (a Mohawk verb conjugator), the WordWeaver web interface, and a Nunavut Hansard translation dataset. The project explicitly adopted an empowerment paradigm (communities control their language tools) that aligns with the IndigenousAI project's values. Cadotte et al. (2022) documented an Innu-Aimun NLP pipeline (FST + neural NMT), reporting 82–87% OOV rates in standard text corpora — a baseline for understanding the scale of the OOV problem for other polysynthetic languages including Mohawk. Le and Sadat (2021) showed that a morphological segmenter combined with NMT substantially outperforms SOTA for Inuktitut and Inuinnaqtun, and Le et al. (2022) demonstrated that deep learning Transformer-based segmentation outperforms FST/Morfessor/Adaptor Grammar approaches for Innu-Aimun. Ngoc et al. (2021) developed the first unsupervised morphological segmentation system for Inuinnaqtun using Adaptor Grammars with linguistic priors, producing a 1,055-word gold evaluation standard. McGiff and Nikolov (2025), synthesizing 54 studies, identified data augmentation strategies (especially back-translation and cross-lingual transfer) as the dominant technical pathway for improving LRL generative modelling, with inconsistent evaluation as the field's core methodological weakness. Mager et al. (2023) provided a comprehensive NLP survey for Indigenous languages of the Americas, documenting specific challenges under ELR conditions.

### RAG, Knowledge Graphs & Retrieval Systems

A substantial cluster of papers addresses retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), knowledge graphs, and the systems-level architecture for grounding LLM outputs in external knowledge bases. These papers are relevant to the IndigenousAI project's potential deployment of a Mohawk knowledge retrieval system — whether RAG-based, GraphRAG-based, or hybrid.

**RAG architecture fundamentals.** The R³AG workshop papers (Wang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025) framed the research agenda: standard RAG fails on complex multi-hop queries; reliable RAG requires addressing intent comprehension, complex document parsing, robust retrieval, and faithful response generation as distinct challenges. Mei et al. (2025), in a comprehensive survey of 1,400+ papers on context engineering, placed RAG in a broader taxonomy that includes memory systems, tool use, and multi-agent architectures, identifying the understanding-generation asymmetry (LLMs understand context better than they generate it) as a foundational design challenge. Wang et al. (2025, CoRAG) demonstrated that training LLMs to perform iterative retrieval chains — decomposing complex queries into sub-queries, retrieving for each, and reformulating on failure — via rejection sampling achieves more than 10 EM points improvement over strong baselines on multi-hop QA and establishes new SOTA on KILT. Salemi and Zamani (2024) provided the evaluation framework: eRAG, which scores each retrieved document individually by running the LLM on it alone, achieves +0.168 to +0.494 Kendall's τ correlation with downstream RAG performance at up to 50× less GPU memory than end-to-end evaluation — a practically important finding for resource-constrained projects.

**GraphRAG and knowledge graph integration.** Peng et al. (2025) provided a comprehensive GraphRAG survey, organizing the field along three axes: G-Indexing (constructing knowledge graphs from text), G-Retrieval (traversing knowledge graphs to assemble context), and G-Generation (using structured graph context in generation). Han et al. (2026) provided an empirical benchmark: flat RAG consistently outperforms GraphRAG on single-hop factoid QA, while GraphRAG outperforms flat RAG on multi-hop reasoning requiring relational inference across entities; hybrid approaches (combining both) outperform either alone. Dehal et al. (2025) surveyed LLM-knowledge graph integration from the opposite direction — how existing knowledge graphs can enrich LLMs — identifying bidirectional enrichment as the emerging paradigm: KGs improve LLM factual grounding, while LLMs assist KG construction and completion.

**Cross-lingual and low-resource RAG.** The Arabzadeh et al. (2025) IR-RAG workshop explicitly identified cross-lingual retrieval (English query → non-English documents, or vice versa) as an underexplored research direction in multilingual RAG — precisely the scenario for a Mohawk-English bilingual knowledge base. No paper in this cluster directly evaluated RAG for Indigenous or polysynthetic languages, leaving a substantial gap.

### Low-Resource LLM Adaptation & Community AI

The newest cluster of papers addresses the practical challenge of adapting capable LLMs to low-resource language settings — either through continued pre-training (CPT), instruction fine-tuning, or curriculum learning — and deploying the resulting models for community benefit.

Pinhanez et al. (2024) demonstrated the closest existing analogue to the IndigenousAI project: fine-tuning SOTA LLMs (including NLLB-200) with small amounts of Nheengatu and Guarani Mbya text produced substantially better translation than zero-shot, and Indigenous Language Models (ILMs) fine-tuned on small corpora powered functional spell-checkers and writing assistants deployed to community members in Brazil. Critically, Pinhanez et al. explicitly cited Mohawk (~3,900 speakers) as within the target range for their approach, establishing a direct reference point. Their community engagement development cycle — needs assessment before technical roadmap, measurement by actual community adoption — directly parallels what the IndigenousAI project should pursue. Godwin-Jones (2025) reinforced the pedagogical dimension: AI tools for less commonly taught languages must account for place-language connections, support pedagogical translanguaging (drawing on learners' full linguistic repertoire), and integrate into existing human teaching practice rather than substituting for it.

Uemura et al. (2024, AfriInstruct) demonstrated that continual pretraining followed by instruction fine-tuning on a curated multilingual dataset enables a single LLaMA-2-7B model to outperform GPT-3.5-Turbo across 19 African languages — establishing a two-stage pipeline as viable at 7B-parameter scale for linguistically diverse low-resource language groups. Yu et al. (2026, AfriqueLLM), from the same Mila-McGill institutional home as IndigenousAI, provided the most rigorous empirical study: CPT on 26B tokens across 20 African languages with systematic variation in data mixture reveals that **data composition is the strongest driver of CPT gains** — adding math, code, and synthetic translated data consistently improves performance more than scaling model size, and architectural choice dominates over scale when comparing across model families. Uemura et al. (2026, MERLIN) introduced a curriculum framework for cross-lingual reasoning that trains a mapping layer to align a multilingual encoder to a frozen LLM through a staged curriculum (general bitext → math bitext → task QA), achieving +12.9 pp over SOTA on low-resource African language mathematical reasoning. Dennison et al. (2026), drawing on 8 deployments across 7 countries and 18 languages, found that human labor (co-design facilitation, translation, community liaison) consistently dominated technical effort in deployed community AI systems — a finding that directly challenges assumptions about AI scalability for community language applications.

### Methodology & Co-construction

The reviewed literature produced a convergent normative framework for ethical research with Indigenous and marginalized communities, though it drew on different traditions and operationalized the principles at different levels of concreteness. Bird (2020) provided the most comprehensive critique and the most operational alternative: building genuine relationships before any data collection or tool development, identifying community goals from the ground up, treating community knowledge holders as authorities rather than data sources, and measuring success by local capability expansion rather than system performance. Liu et al. (2022) concretized this into six actionable recommendations: build community bonds first; offer co-authorship to community contributors; document data collection challenges transparently; clarify data ownership including copyright for Master Speakers; create technology jointly; and plan for how technology output integrates into existing revitalization work. Cooper et al. (2024), drawing on interviews with seventeen researchers working with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, proposed a cyclical engagement process: seek community needs, engage representative bodies, negotiate control, create community benefit, store data accessibly, and scale tools as "digital shells" adaptable by other communities.

Brinklow et al. (2019) and Brinklow (2021) contributed the most directly Canada- and Mohawk-specific normative framework. Writing from a Mohawk community standpoint, they argued that digital colonization — the structural exclusion of Indigenous languages from the global technology ecosystem — makes Indigenous leadership in language technology development a political necessity, not merely a best practice. The "anti-colonial oases" framing — building digital spaces where Mohawk language is centered — directly defines the project goal for IndigenousAI. Their "technology as MacGuffin" critique warned against enthusiasm for AI tools as the solution: the question is not "can we build a Mohawk LLM?" but "does this technology actually serve what the community is trying to achieve?" Meighan (2021) extended the decolonial analysis across thirty years of Indigenous language revitalization technology, identifying six technology types and arguing that technology is never neutral — the relationship between technology developer and community encodes assumptions about who has authority, what knowledge is valuable, and whose goals are served. Herrera et al. (2022), writing from a Mapuche scholar's perspective, reinforced that language revitalization is inseparable from identity and self-determination: technology that treats language as an object of study rather than a living expression of identity fails on the project's own terms.

Junker (2024) provided the most urgent data sovereignty analysis: documenting three "data mining events" on Algonquian language databases and the AI gold rush on rare language data, Junker argued that the existing OCAP® framework (Ownership, Control, Access, Possession) is insufficient for the AI era and that a specific code of conduct governing AI use of Indigenous language materials is urgently needed. This finding directly affects the IndigenousAI project's use of existing Mohawk language databases and corpora. Pinhanez and Wornyo (2025) operationalized co-development ethics into a practical curriculum covering trust building, relational accountability, data control frameworks (OCAP®, CARE), decolonial licensing (the Kaitiakitanga model), and co-design vs. co-development distinctions — providing the most actionable practical guidance in the corpus for structuring the IndigenousAI project's community engagement.

A finding that cut across all foundational methodology papers was that community members universally prioritized cultural transmission across generations as the goal of language technology — not linguistic documentation, digital preservation, or benchmark performance. This goal orientation had direct methodological consequences: communities wanted technology that enabled learning between humans, not technology that substituted for human transmission.

### Evaluation & Benchmarking

The evaluation literature revealed a field whose measurement frameworks were falling behind its technical ambitions on three distinct fronts: cultural alignment evaluation for general-purpose LLMs, linguistic reasoning evaluation for low-resource and unseen languages, and translation quality evaluation specifically designed for Indigenous languages.

On the cultural alignment front, Adilazuarda et al. (2024) documented that black-box probing with culturally conditioned prompts suffered from profound prompt sensitivity. Khan et al. (2025) provided the most systematic empirical demonstration of this instability: across established cultural surveys, LLM cultural preferences shifted by more than one standard deviation of real between-country human variation when only presentation format was changed. Branco et al. (2026) identified three structural flaws beyond instability: reliance on externally imposed, stereotyped knowledge; focus on encyclopedic facts widely available in training data; and the "oracle effect," where explicit cultural identifiers inflated scores by up to forty-two percentage points. Sakhaeirad et al. (2026) revealed a related failure mode through the DIVANBENCH benchmark for Persian: models exhibited severe acquiescence bias, accepting culturally appropriate statements at 84–92% accuracy while rejecting clear violations at only 19–48%. Bhagat et al. (2026), through large-scale annotation with 108 cultural experts, found that 88% of LLM-generated stories contained at least one cultural misrepresentation — averaging 5.42 per story — and that misrepresentations quadrupled for low-resource languages.

On the linguistic reasoning front for low-resource and unseen languages, Bean et al. (2024) developed LingOly from UK Linguistics Olympiad material: 1,133 olympiad-level linguistic puzzles across 90+ mostly low-resource or extinct languages. The best-performing model (Claude Opus) achieved 46.3% exact match overall and only 38.7% on harder Round 2 problems. Sánchez et al. (2025) developed Linguini from International Linguistics Olympiad material, covering 894 questions in 75 mostly XLR languages; state-of-the-art LLMs scored below 25%, with a large gap between proprietary and open-source models. Zhu et al. (2025) used a related IOL-style Rosetta Stone framework to show that step-by-step decomposition of linguistic puzzles dramatically improved performance, with Claude 3.5 Sonnet exceeding average human performance on translation into English in the step-by-step condition.

The third front — evaluation metrics designed specifically for Indigenous languages — was addressed directly by Krasner et al. (2025), the first paper in the corpus to target this gap for the languages most relevant to the project. Standard MT metrics (BLEU, ChrF++) fail for Indigenous languages because their polysynthetic morphology, extensive morphological variation, and non-standardized spelling break lexical and character-level matching assumptions. Krasner et al. (2025) fine-tuned LaBSE specifically for Guarani, Bribri, and Nahuatl, finding that layer-averaged YiSi-1 using the fine-tuned embeddings achieved the highest average correlation with human judgment among submitted systems in the AmericasNLP 2025 metrics shared task. Dennison et al. (2026) introduced a complementary framework: the LISTED evaluation model for deployed community AI systems, which assesses cultural alignment through community-defined criteria rather than researcher-defined benchmarks, and found that human labor consistently dominates technical effort in practice. Salemi and Zamani (2024) provided a new retrieval evaluation dimension: eRAG's finding that human relevance judgments show only minor correlation with actual RAG performance suggests that community-curated evaluation datasets need to be designed for LLM utility, not just linguistic relevance.

The finding that "language does not equal culture" emerged from multiple directions. Zhang et al. (2025), using CultureScope, showed that additional multilingual pretraining did not yield more cultural knowledge. Feng et al. (2025) found cross-language inconsistency in culturally specific responses. Monazzah et al. (2025) showed that Persian fine-tuned models underperformed their base models on cultural tasks. These results collectively warned against treating Mohawk-language pretraining as a proxy for cultural competence, and against relying on English-mediated evaluation of Mohawk cultural outputs.

## Convergences

- **WEIRD bias is pervasive and measurable across evaluation frameworks.** Multiple papers independently documented that LLMs and their evaluation benchmarks disproportionately represent Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic cultures, to the systematic exclusion of Indigenous, African, and other non-WEIRD communities (Adilazuarda et al., 2024; Pawar et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2025; Khan et al., 2025).

- **Cultural knowledge in LLMs does not straightforwardly translate into cultural competence in generation.** The knowledge-generation gap (Bhagat et al., 2026), the factual-conceptual gap (Sakhaeirad et al., 2026), the surface-vs.-deep politeness discrepancy (Sadr et al., 2025), and the cultural degradation under language-switching (Monazzah et al., 2025) all established independently that factual retrieval accuracy was a poor predictor of culturally appropriate generative behavior.

- **Community co-construction and process quality are prerequisites, not optional additions.** Bird (2020), Cooper et al. (2024), Liu et al. (2022), Zhang et al. (2022), Zhao et al. (2026), Brinklow et al. (2019), Brinklow (2021), Pinhanez and Wornyo (2025), Meighan (2021), and Herrera et al. (2022) all concluded, from different empirical and political bases, that the ethical and practical quality of language technology for marginalized communities depended more on engagement process than on technical outputs — and that process failures reproduced colonial harms regardless of system performance.

- **Dictionaries are the highest-value in-context resource for XLR language translation, consistently outperforming grammar descriptions used alone.** Tanzer et al. (2024), Aycock et al. (2025), Pei et al. (2025), Court and Elsner (2024), Zhang et al. (2024, LINGOLLM), Lu et al. (2024), and Zhang et al. (2024, DIPMTT++) all converged on the finding that morpheme-level dictionary entries and retrieved parallel examples drove translation gains, while unstructured grammar prose contributed little or negatively.

- **Structured or modular presentation of grammar knowledge substantially improves ICL translation.** Where unstructured grammar books failed, structured alternatives succeeded: Guo et al.'s (2024) TALENT textbook prompting achieved 14.8% gains by having the model explicitly extract syntax patterns before translation, and Zhang et al. (2025) achieved 13.1% BLEU gains by formatting grammar rules as pseudo-code and decomposing translation into explicit retrieval and application steps.

- **Standard NLP metrics are insufficient for evaluating culturally and linguistically complex outputs.** ChrF over BLEU for morphologically rich languages (Ebrahimi et al., 2023; de Gibert et al., 2025), fine-tuned semantic embedding metrics for Indigenous languages (Krasner et al., 2025), CSI-recall for cultural fidelity (Ansari et al., 2026), scenario-based over politeness-classifier evaluation (Sadr et al., 2025), eRAG over human relevance judgments for retrieval (Salemi and Zamani, 2024), and thick over thin evaluation (Qadri et al., 2025) all pointed to the same conclusion.

- **Polysynthetic morphology poses distinct challenges for standard NLP pipelines.** Zhang et al. (2022), Ebrahimi et al. (2023), de Gibert et al. (2025), Rai and Pal (2025), Haddow et al. (2022), Mager et al. (2022, 2023), Khandagale et al. (2022), and Zhu et al. (2025) collectively established that standard subword tokenization failed on morphologically complex languages, that rule-based or morphological-grounding approaches often outperformed pure LLM prompting, and that LLMs exhibited systematic typological bias against morphological structures deviating from patterns dominant in high-resource training data.

- **Morphological alignment of tokenizers does not predict downstream LM performance.** Arnett and Bergen (2025) and Arnett et al. (2025) both found no significant correlation between MorphScore and model perplexity across dozens of languages. Data disparity (exacerbated by the byte premium) is the dominant explanation for the performance gap between morphologically complex and morphologically simple languages, not tokenizer misalignment.

- **Data composition — not model scale — is the dominant driver of low-resource LLM adaptation gains.** Yu et al. (2026, AfriqueLLM) established empirically that adding math, code, and synthetic translated data to a CPT corpus consistently outperforms scaling model size or using a larger base model with stronger multilingual coverage. This finding implies that curation strategy matters more than compute for the IndigenousAI project.

- **Fine-tuning with tiny Indigenous language data can produce functional tools.** Pinhanez et al. (2024) demonstrated that fine-tuning NLLB-200 with small amounts of Nheengatu and Guarani Mbya text produced substantially better translation and enabled ILMs powering community-deployed spell-checkers and writing assistants. Yahan and Islam (2025) showed LoRA fine-tuning of a 3B model achieves meaningful Indigenous language sentence transformation. These findings collectively establish that functional tools are achievable at current Mohawk data volumes.

- **RAG outperforms GraphRAG on single-hop queries; GraphRAG outperforms RAG on multi-hop queries; hybrid approaches outperform either alone.** Han et al. (2026), Peng et al. (2025), and the CoRAG paper (Wang et al., 2025) collectively established a nuanced architecture landscape. For a Mohawk knowledge retrieval system, the dominant query type — factoid lookup vs. relational inference — should determine the primary architecture.

- **Data sovereignty and community control over language resources are recognized as necessary conditions for ethical AI development.** Kay et al. (2024), Cooper et al. (2024), Liu et al. (2022), Zhao et al. (2026), Liu et al. (2025), Junker (2024), Brinklow et al. (2019), Brinklow (2021), and Pinhanez and Wornyo (2025) explicitly named data sovereignty as a prerequisite for ethical Indigenous language AI, citing frameworks such as CARE, OCAP®, and decolonial licensing models.

- **Representational gaps compound for smaller localities and lower-resource communities.** Bhagat et al. (2026), Singh et al. (2025), Ebrahimi et al. (2023), Khanuja et al. (2026), Song et al. (2026), and Tonja et al. (2024) all found that performance degraded significantly for communities, regions, and languages with smaller training-data footprints, a pattern implying the situation for Indigenous languages will be worse, not merely analogous, relative to mid-resource settings.

## Tensions & Divergences

- **Co-construction as principle vs. co-construction as practice.** Bird (2020), Cooper et al. (2024), and Liu et al. (2022) all advocated for genuine community co-design, but their specific protocols, power structures, and community authority frameworks differed. These frameworks are complementary in intent but potentially divergent in practice — particularly regarding which community members hold authority, how disagreements within communities are handled, and what counts as adequate reciprocity. The new ICL and prompting papers deepened this tension by operating entirely outside any co-construction framework while making direct use of community-originated linguistic materials.

- **Whether grammar descriptions help or hinder LLM translation.** Zhang et al. (2024, LINGOLLM) found that morphological analyzers and grammar books, combined in a pipeline, substantially improved translation performance. Tanzer et al. (2024), Aycock et al. (2025), Pei et al. (2025), and Court and Elsner (2024) found that grammar descriptions provided as raw in-context material contributed little to translation quality and sometimes harmed it. Guo et al. (2024) found that structured textbook prompting with explicit syntax pattern extraction produced consistent gains, and Zhang et al. (2025) identified rule retrieval rather than rule comprehension as the bottleneck — suggesting the contradiction is resolvable: structured, modular, code-formatted grammar materials help, while unstructured prose grammar books used as bulk context do not.

- **Morphological alignment as tokenizer design goal.** Mager et al. (2022) showed that unsupervised morphological segmentation outperformed BPE for ELR NMT, suggesting morphological alignment matters for translation. Arnett and Bergen (2025) and Arnett et al. (2025) showed that MorphScore does not predict LM perplexity, suggesting alignment does not matter for language modeling. Schmidt et al. (2024) showed that maximizing compression (PathPiece) does not help even when compression correlates with performance at the cross-tokenizer level (Goldman et al., 2024). The resolution may be task-specific: morphological alignment matters for MT but not for LM perplexity; compression matters for LM pretraining but not within a fixed vocabulary.

- **Transliteration as workaround vs. tokenizer training as principled solution.** Ma et al. (2025) demonstrated that Latin transliteration substantially improved ICL performance for non-Latin script languages — up to 25% F1 gains for NER — while acknowledging this was a workaround for tokenization fragmentation rather than a principled fix. Li et al. (2025) reinforced the problem: PEFT degraded performance on unseen-script languages precisely because it updated weights without correcting vocabulary coverage. Rai and Pal (2025) and Zhang et al. (2022) both pointed toward language-specific tokenizer training as the theoretically sound approach. For Mohawk (Latin script), the script-specific failure mode did not apply, but the tension between quick-workaround and principled-solution remained relevant.

- **Whether increasing language-specific training data helps or hurts.** Sakhaeirad et al. (2026) found that continued Persian-language pretraining degraded rejection accuracy by forty-three percentage points. Feng et al. (2025) demonstrated gains from target-aware multilingual critique fine-tuning. Zhang et al. (2025) showed that additional multilingual pretraining did not improve cultural knowledge. Song et al. (2026) showed that distillation from a teacher model using monolingual LRL data substantially improved translation without catastrophic forgetting. Yu et al. (2026, AfriqueLLM) demonstrated consistent gains from CPT with well-curated data mixtures. These results may reflect differences in evaluation design, model size, and the specific type of cultural or linguistic knowledge being assessed.

- **Quantitative benchmarking vs. thick participatory evaluation.** Khan et al. (2025) argued that survey-based cultural alignment evaluation was fundamentally unreliable. Bhagat et al. (2026), Monazzah et al. (2025), Bean et al. (2024), Sánchez et al. (2025), and Krasner et al. (2025) invested substantial effort in building or improving quantitative benchmarks. Qadri et al. (2025) positioned thick participatory evaluation as a necessary alternative to any quantitative approach, while Branco et al. (2026) and Singh et al. (2025) argued that better-designed quantitative benchmarks were the right response.

- **The role of fine-tuning for cultural adaptation.** Sadr et al. (2025) demonstrated that DPO fine-tuning on 450 taarof scenarios produced a 42.3-point accuracy gain approaching native speaker performance. Monazzah et al. (2025) found that Persian-fine-tuned models underperformed their base models on cultural tasks. Feng et al. (2025) showed gains from critique-driven fine-tuning but only for well-resourced cultural contexts. Yahan and Islam (2025) achieved meaningful sentence transformation results for Indigenous languages via LoRA fine-tuning but without community evaluation. Yu et al. (2026) showed consistent CPT gains with careful data curation.

- **Whether technology fundamentally serves or threatens Indigenous language revitalization.** Bird (2020) and Meighan (2021) argued that technology had not produced evidence of reversing language shift and that its extractive logic actively risked re-enacting colonial harm. Liu et al. (2022), Zhang et al. (2022), and Pinhanez et al. (2024) were more optimistic, showing communities that did express specific technology needs and deployed tools seeing actual adoption. The XLR translation and ICL papers implicitly adopted the optimistic framing — building tools first without first building community relationships — which Bird (2020) and Liu et al. (2022) would identify as methodologically prior failure.

- **How to handle restricted cultural knowledge in AI systems.** Branco et al. (2026) proposed that cultural benchmarks should include only knowledge that community insiders would share without concern, but gave no guidance on how to identify that boundary or enforce it technically. Zhao et al. (2026) explicitly raised data sovereignty as requiring that flagged examples from auditing remain under community control. Junker (2024) documented cases where AI systems were trained on restricted community language data without consent, establishing this as an active harm rather than a hypothetical risk.

## Gaps & Open Questions

- **Indigenous languages, including Mohawk, are entirely absent from the cultural alignment evaluation benchmarks reviewed.** No paper in this set included an Indigenous language from the Americas in a cultural alignment benchmark. The construction of community-grounded cultural evaluation resources for Kanien'kéha, informed by Six Nations knowledge holders, is identified as a need but not addressed anywhere in the reviewed literature.

- **The grammar-book and dictionary-augmented paradigms have not been applied to polysynthetic languages or evaluated by speaker communities.** Tanzer et al. (2024), Aycock et al. (2025), Pei et al. (2025), Zhang et al. (2025), Lu et al. (2024), and Zhang et al. (2024, DIPMTT++) all tested on languages that, while low-resource, did not exhibit the extreme morphological complexity of polysynthetic languages like Mohawk. A particular challenge for dictionary-augmented approaches is sparse dictionary coverage for inflected forms: polysynthetic morphology generates a vast space of verb forms, most of which will not appear in any bilingual lexicon.

- **The ICL-to-MT bootstrapping pipeline has not been validated for polysynthetic languages.** Pei et al. (2025) demonstrated that ICL-generated synthetic parallel data could train a conventional MT model, and Song et al. (2026) showed distillation from teacher pseudo-translations was viable with monolingual data. Neither study included a polysynthetic language, and both relied on automated evaluation metrics. Whether a teacher LLM with minimal Mohawk pretraining can generate pseudo-translations of sufficient quality to serve as training data — and who owns those pseudo-translations — remained open.

- **Evaluation metrics for Indigenous language MT remain underdeveloped, with Mohawk specifically unaddressed.** Krasner et al. (2025) partially addressed the evaluation gap by demonstrating that fine-tuned semantic embedding metrics substantially outperformed BLEU/ChrF++ for Guarani, Bribri, and Nahuatl. However, Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee languages were not among the languages studied. The fine-tuning approach required aligned sentence pairs and human judgment data that remain scarce for Mohawk.

- **Educational applications of LLM tools for polysynthetic Indigenous languages remain undemonstrated.** Yahan and Islam (2025) provided a proof-of-concept for sentence transformation as an educational tool for Bribri, Guarani, and Maya, but Mohawk's verb-centric polysynthetic morphology makes transformation tasks structurally more complex than for the languages tested.

- **The minimum viable data volume for CPT to produce meaningful improvement at Mohawk's resource level is unknown.** Yu et al. (2026) studied 20 African languages; even the lowest-resource language had ~90M tokens — far more than the estimated few million tokens of available Mohawk text. Whether the CPT pipeline scales down to Mohawk data volumes, and what the minimum viable corpus looks like, has not been established.

- **The FST-bootstrapped LLM segmentation pipeline (Stenlund et al., 2025) has not been applied to Mohawk.** The UQAILAUT-based pipeline for Inuktitut could be replicated using Kawennón:nis (NRC Canada's Mohawk verb conjugator) as the FST annotator for Mohawk verb segmentation. This represents a tractable near-term contribution: the pieces exist but have not been assembled for Mohawk.

- **Cross-lingual RAG for Indigenous languages is entirely unexplored.** Arabzadeh et al. (2025) and Wang et al. (2024) both identified cross-lingual retrieval as an underexplored frontier. For a Mohawk-English bilingual knowledge base, retrieval across languages (English query → Mohawk documents, or Mohawk query → English cultural knowledge) is the natural use case, but no empirical work on this scenario exists for any Indigenous language.

- **Decolonial licensing and community IP frameworks for AI-generated content do not yet exist for Haudenosaunee contexts.** Pinhanez and Wornyo (2025) documented the Kaitiakitanga (Māori guardianship) license as a decolonial software licensing model. No equivalent framework exists for Haudenosaunee communities. Under what license would a Mohawk-adapted model, trained on community-provided data, be released, and who would control its deployment?

- **Polysynthetic language challenges for generative AI remain underexplored.** While Zhang et al. (2022), Ebrahimi et al. (2023), de Gibert et al. (2025), and Zhu et al. (2025) documented the failure of standard tokenization and prompting for polysynthetic morphology, no paper proposed a theoretically grounded alternative architecture or training paradigm specifically for polysynthetic language generation. The implications of polysynthesis for LLM pretraining, alignment, and evaluation remained open.

- **Linguistic reasoning benchmarks (LingOly, Linguini) likely do not include Haudenosaunee languages.** Bean et al. (2024) and Sánchez et al. (2025) provided principled, contamination-resistant evaluation frameworks for linguistic reasoning in XLR languages, drawing on UKLO and ILO puzzle material respectively. Neither confirmed coverage of Mohawk, Cayuga, Seneca, or other Haudenosaunee languages; LingOly explicitly excludes non-Latin scripts but Mohawk uses the Latin script and would be eligible for inclusion. Authoring UKLO/ILO-style morphological puzzles for Kanien'kéha, with Six Nations community linguist involvement, was identified as both a need and a tractable contribution.

- **Data sovereignty and community consent in XLR translation pipelines remain unresolved.** Court and Elsner (2024), Song et al. (2026), and Junker (2024) identified the problem from different angles; Junker documented three specific incidents of unauthorized data mining on Algonquian language databases. No paper in the reviewed set provided an operational framework for community-controlled XLR translation development, where communities own the grammar tools, the parallel data, the fine-tuned model, and the generated outputs.

- **The minimum viable parallel corpus threshold for usable XLR translation is unclear.** Zhang et al. (2024, DIPMTT++) used 5K parallel sentences. Mohawk currently lacks 5K high-quality parallel sentences, and the practical floor for meaningful translation assistance — including with CoD, TALENT, or DIPMTT++ approaches — has not been established for languages at Mohawk's resource level.

- **The "acquiescence bias" and factual-conceptual gap have only been studied for Persian and Korean.** Sakhaeirad et al. (2026) documented the catastrophic failure of models to reject cultural norm violations — but studies cited a single non-WEIRD language. Whether these failure modes manifest for languages with very sparse training-data presence (such as Mohawk, where even cultural facts are largely absent from pretraining corpora) was not investigated.

- **The relationship between oral tradition and AI system design is theoretically underdeveloped.** Bird (2020) and Liu et al. (2022) both emphasized the primacy of oral tradition in Indigenous language revitalization, and Ansari et al. (2026) demonstrated gains from incorporating audio modality in NMT. But no paper investigated how AI systems could be designed to support oral-first learning paradigms — where the target output is spoken-language competence acquired through human relationships — rather than merely processing transcriptions of oral content.

- **Community-controlled fine-tuning and alignment pipelines do not yet exist for endangered-language settings.** Barman et al. (2025) proposed making RLHF more pluralistic, Sadr et al. (2025) demonstrated that DPO on small datasets could close large cultural competence gaps, Yahan and Islam (2025) demonstrated LoRA fine-tuning on limited hardware for Indigenous language tasks, and Feng et al. (2025) showed critique-driven fine-tuning gains — but all of these approaches assumed access to existing datasets or LLM infrastructure that communities could neither own nor audit. A pipeline for community-controlled alignment, where Indigenous community members define the reward signal and retain ownership of training data, did not exist in the reviewed literature.

- **Evaluation of "missingness" — what the model cannot say — remains methodologically underdeveloped.** Qadri et al. (2025) identified missingness (important cultural elements absent from outputs) as one of the five dimensions of cultural misrepresentation most important to community members, and noted that standard benchmarks were entirely blind to it. For endangered languages undergoing revitalization, missingness may be the dominant failure mode: a model generating Mohawk language content may fail not by producing wrong forms but by being unable to produce culturally significant forms at all.

- **The intersection of gender, kinship, and clan structures in Indigenous language AI remains unaddressed.** In Haudenosaunee communities, gender, clan, and kinship relationships are structurally encoded in language through verb morphology, forms of address, and social obligations, and carry political significance distinct from Western gender categories. No paper in the reviewed set examined how colonial-era gender and kinship frameworks embedded in LLM training data would interact with Indigenous linguistic and social structures, or how to detect and mitigate such harms.
