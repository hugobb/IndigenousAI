# Preserving Linguistic Diversity in the Digital Age: A Scalable Model

**Authors:** James Hutson, Pace Ellsworth, Matt Ellsworth
**Year:** 2024
**Venue:** Journal of Contemporary Language Research, Vol. 3, No. 1

---

## Core Argument

Linguistic diversity faces a dual crisis: language extinction is accelerating (one language every 40 days since 2000; ~3,000 languages projected to disappear by 2100), and the rise of English-dominant AI systems — particularly large language models developed by major U.S. tech companies — is exacerbating the erosion of minority languages. The authors propose a comprehensive, scalable model for cultural heritage and linguistic preservation that leverages machine learning and big data analytics, arguing that technology must be deployed to counteract the homogenizing tendencies of its own dominant form.

## Key Concepts

- **English dominance in LLMs:** Major LLMs (GPT series, etc.) are predominantly trained on English data; their deployment as global communication tools reinforces English as the dominant cognitive and cultural paradigm, marginalizing languages with less digital presence.
- **Orality vs. literacy shift:** The rise of conversational AI (voice interfaces, chat) is driving a broader cultural shift from text-based literacy to oral communication patterns. This has mixed implications for language diversity — orality is the natural medium for many Indigenous languages, but AI-mediated orality is English-centric.
- **Language loss as cognitive and cultural loss:** Each language represents a unique cognitive framework, cultural worldview, and accumulated knowledge system; language loss is irreversible loss of human intellectual diversity.
- **Scalable preservation model:** The proposed model uses ML and big data to document and archive diverse cultural artifacts at scale — moving beyond narrow niche preservation to comprehensive "everyday" cultural documentation.
- **Lingua franca reform:** The authors argue for a "more meaningful and comprehensive lingua franca" as a prerequisite for equitable multilingual AI — though this remains underspecified.
- **7,151 languages globally:** Of these, ~700 have been lost since 1900, and the pace is accelerating.

## Main Findings

- Since 2000, languages have been disappearing at approximately one every 40 days, or nine per year; projections suggest ~3,000 languages lost by 2100 at current rates.
- English-centric LLM development poses a structural risk to linguistic diversity: by making English the most effective medium for interacting with AI, these systems create economic and social incentives to abandon minority languages.
- The shift toward orality facilitated by NLP (voice interfaces, conversational AI) has both positive potential (oral traditions can be recorded and modeled) and negative potential (AI-mediated orality is English-centric).
- A scalable ML-based preservation model needs to document not just prestige linguistic artifacts but everyday cultural expressions, which carry most of the language's vitality.
- Bilingualism and multilingualism confer cognitive benefits (improved executive function, delayed dementia onset, neuroplasticity) that are lost with language death — framing language preservation as a public health issue as well as a cultural one.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

Hutson et al. provide useful framing for the macro-level case for the IndigenousAI project: language loss is not just a cultural loss but a cognitive and public health loss, and AI development as currently practiced is accelerating that loss for minority languages. The English-LLM critique is directly applicable to Mohawk — any foundation model used as a base for Mohawk language support will have been trained predominantly on English, structurally disadvantaging Mohawk. The orality angle is interesting: Mohawk is a primarily oral language with a rich tradition of oral transmission (ceremonial speeches, oral histories), and AI tools that support audio recording, transcription, and retrieval could serve orality rather than undermine it. The scalable preservation model is less directly applicable — the project's focus is on language use and revitalization, not archiving.

## Limitations & Critiques

- The paper's proposed "scalable model" remains at a high level of abstraction; it does not specify concrete methods, architectures, or evaluation criteria.
- The authors are not linguists or Indigenous scholars; the paper lacks depth on community-specific considerations, data sovereignty, or community agency in language preservation.
- The "lingua franca reform" proposal is politically naive and underspecified — it is not clear what alternative global communication system is envisioned or how it would be achieved.
- The paper reviews language loss statistics and AI limitations at a fairly surface level; practitioners working in the field will find more actionable guidance in domain-specific literature (Brinklow, Godwin-Jones, Cadotte, etc.).

## Questions & Follow-ups

- Are there existing ML-based large-scale language documentation projects that have implemented something like the proposed scalable model? What are the outcomes?
- How does the orality-to-literacy shift specifically affect Indigenous oral traditions — are there projects using speech recognition and audio AI to support oral language transmission?
- Related work: Brinklow et al. (2019) on language reclamation; Ajani et al. (2024) on digital media for preservation; Godwin-jones (2025) on AI and language pedagogy; Herrera (2022) on technology and identity.
