# Ethical Co-Development of AI Applications with Indigenous Communities

**Authors:** Claudio Santos Pinhanez (IBM Research Brazil), Edem Wornyo (Google Research)
**Year:** 2025
**Venue:** Extended Abstracts of CHI 2025 (Course description)

---

## Core Argument

Working with Indigenous communities to develop AI applications is not merely a technical challenge but an ethical, relational, and political one. This CHI 2025 course — taught by practitioners who have conducted community-based AI work in Brazil (IBM Research + USP, Nheengatu speakers) and globally (Google Research, worldwide Indigenous communities) — covers the practical knowledge needed to engage ethically: from initial partnering principles and legal constraints through data sovereignty, co-design methods, and sustainable technology transfer.

## Key Concepts

- **Trust building:** Communities have deep and justified distrust of corporate bodies based on a history of extractive collaborations; building trust requires demonstrated empathy, consistent action over time, and transparent accountability — not just project kickoff agreements.
- **Relational accountability:** Being accountable to the community as a partner, not just to funders or academic review boards. Decisions are made with communities, not about them.
- **Data control and sovereignty:** Indigenous communities retain control over their language and cultural data; informed consent must be free, prior, and ongoing — not a one-time signature. Data control frameworks (OCAP®, CARE principles) should govern data collection and storage.
- **Decolonial software licensing:** The Māori license "Kaitiakitanga" (Guardianship License, New Zealand) is presented as a case study — a software license that restricts usage to beneficial purposes as defined by the community, representing a decolonial approach to IP.
- **Co-design vs. co-development:** Co-design involves communities in determining what to build (requirements, priorities); co-development involves them in how it is built (design iterations, evaluation). Both are necessary; neither alone is sufficient.
- **Technology transfer and appropriation:** Sustainable deployment requires ensuring the community can maintain, adapt, and transfer technology to their own members without ongoing dependence on the external development team.
- **Being vs. Doing the Indigenous way:** "Being the Indigenous way" means building relationships and mutual understanding before jumping to technical solutions; "Doing the Indigenous way" means using culturally appropriate methods and respecting community decision-making processes.

## Main Findings (from course content)

- Communities' past extractive experiences create legitimate barriers; these must be acknowledged and addressed through relationship-building before technical engagement begins.
- Standard HCI development models (sprint-based, user-centered design) are often incompatible with Indigenous decision-making timescales and community governance processes.
- "Usage-focused development models" — where the development cycle is driven by actual community use cases and adoption, not research milestones — are more appropriate for Indigenous contexts.
- Intellectual property frameworks in most jurisdictions do not protect Indigenous cultural knowledge; decolonial licensing models (like Kaitiakitanga) are one response, but legal frameworks are still developing.
- Non-extractive AI research requires communities to receive tangible benefits from collaborations, not just publication co-authorship.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

This course provides a practical curriculum for the IndigenousAI project's community engagement approach. The specific cases studied — FOIRN (Federation of Indigenous Organizations of the Rio Negro, Brazil), Nheengatu writing assistant deployment, Māori license — are instructive analogies for the Six Nations context. The "Being vs. Doing the Indigenous way" distinction is directly applicable: the project must invest in the relationship with Six Nations before committing to a technical roadmap. The trust-building section — which documents specific community skepticism about corporate bodies — mirrors what the IndigenousAI project will encounter. The decolonial software licensing concept (Kaitiakitanga) raises an important question: under what license would any Mohawk language AI tool be released? The course instructors (Pinhanez and Wornyo) are among the most experienced practitioners of Indigenous AI co-development globally; their work with Brazilian communities is directly relevant as an analogue.

## Limitations & Critiques

- This is a course abstract (4 pages), not a research paper; it describes content but does not report empirical findings or evaluate outcomes.
- The cases are from Brazil (FOIRN, Nheengatu) and New Zealand (Māori); the North American Indigenous rights framework (UNDRIP, OCAP®, treaty rights) is different in important ways.
- The emphasis on corporate/institutional perspectives (IBM Research, Google Research) may not fully capture what community-led AI development (where the community controls the project, not just participates) would look like.

## Questions & Follow-ups

- How does the Kaitiakitanga license apply in Canadian/US legal contexts? Are there analogous decolonial licensing models for Haudenosaunee/Mohawk communities?
- What governance structure would allow Six Nations/Mohawk communities to actually control (not just participate in) the IndigenousAI project?
