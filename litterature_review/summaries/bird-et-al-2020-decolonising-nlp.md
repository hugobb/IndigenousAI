# Decolonising Speech and Language Technology

**Authors:** Steven Bird
**Year:** 2020
**Venue:** Proceedings of the 28th International Conference on Computational Linguistics (COLING 2020), pages 3504–3519, Barcelona, Spain (Online)

---

## Core Argument

The speech and language technology (SLT) community has reproduced colonial logics in its engagement with Indigenous languages — treating them as data to be extracted, problems to be solved, or codes to be modernized — while largely ignoring Indigenous self-determination. Bird argues for a fundamental reorientation toward participatory, community-driven co-design, where technology supports communities' own goals (which often center on knowledge transmission and wellbeing rather than linguistic documentation), and where evaluation is measured by local agency and capability expansion rather than system performance metrics.

## Key Concepts

- **Language Documentation Mechanism:** The standard pipeline (documentation → orthography/lexicon → literature production → pedagogical resources → language programs) that positions linguists as central agents and speakers as data sources, with community agency largely absent.
- **Zero resource scenario:** The framing of Indigenous languages as if nothing is known about them, enabling fully automated "discovery" methods — a neocolonial move that disenfranchises existing linguists, speakers, and knowledge holders.
- **Gold data ideology:** The preoccupation with extracting sanitized, transcribed, annotated speech corpora, which commodifies Indigenous language as data and creates burdens for communities without serving their actual priorities.
- **"Unwritten language" framing:** Labeling oral languages as deficient ("under-resourced") rather than recognizing that writing is one technology among many, and that oral knowledge transmission has its own validity and richness.
- **Language Continuation Continuum (Bedford & Casson, 2010):** Positions activities from naturalistic community acquisition (left, community-driven) to documentation and archiving (right, researcher-driven); funding and NLP research concentrates on the right while communities need support on the left.
- **FAMED conditions (Lewis & Simons):** For language sustainability: Functions, Acquisition means, Motivation, favorable Environment, and Differentiation from dominant languages. Most NLP tools address only acquisition and environment, neglecting the others.
- **Yarning:** An Indigenous Australian research method (relational, story-based conversation) that Bird frames as a legitimate starting point for participatory technology development — building relationships before agendas.
- **Realist evaluation:** An evaluation framework asking not "did it work?" but "what mechanisms produced what outcomes in what contexts?" — enabling nuanced understanding of how technology interventions interact with community realities.

## Main Findings

- The "technology saves languages" narrative is empirically unsupported: there is scant evidence after 25 years that documentation has played a significant role in reversing language shift.
- Indigenous communities often prioritize knowledge transmission, identity, wellbeing, and connection over linguistic proficiency or documentation — goals that most SLT systems do not address.
- The data-centric approach actively risks re-enacting causes of language endangerment: commodifying languages, disenfranchising knowledge authorities, and creating dependency rather than self-determination.
- Decolonising practices require: building genuine relationships (not extractive engagements), identifying community goals from the ground up, treating speakers as knowledge authorities rather than data sources, prioritizing vehicular languages, supporting oral learning, and mobilizing existing archives rather than perpetually collecting new data.
- Evaluation must be expanded beyond system metrics to include: how much funding reaches Indigenous participants, whether products are locally sustainable, whether local voices are elevated, and whether capabilities are expanded.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

This is the most directly relevant paper in the set for the IndigenousAI project's methodology. Bird's critique of extractive NLP work maps precisely onto risks the Mila project must actively resist: treating Mohawk as "low-resource data" to be processed, centering researcher expertise over Six Nations community authority, and prioritizing documentation over the community's own revitalization goals. The paper's positive framework — co-design, relationship-building, community-driven goal identification, capability expansion as evaluation criterion — provides an operational methodology for the project. The distinction between what the NLP community values (data, systems, metrics) and what Indigenous communities value (knowledge, connection, identity, wellbeing) is foundational for designing any AI-assisted Mohawk language tool. The concept of "yarning" as a research method is directly applicable to community engagement with Six Nations.

## Limitations & Critiques

- Bird acknowledges the irony and discomfort of writing a decolonization paper without an Indigenous co-author — a structural limitation that reflects broader power imbalances in academic publishing.
- The paper's context is primarily Australian Aboriginal communities; while the principles are broadly applicable, the specific dynamics of Haudenosaunee/Six Nations communities in a North American settler-colonial context may differ in important ways.
- The paper does not fully engage with the question of what types of AI tools (if any) are genuinely wanted and requested by specific communities — it is more critical than constructive about concrete use cases.
- The notion of "community goals" risks homogenizing internally diverse communities; communities have multiple voices and competing visions for language work.

## Questions & Follow-ups

- How does the Six Nations/Mohawk community's relationship with technology compare to the Australian Aboriginal communities Bird describes? What are the specific protocols around language sovereignty and data governance for Haudenosaunee communities?
- What would "yarning" as a research method look like in the context of a Mila research partnership with Six Nations — who initiates, who holds authority, what reciprocity is expected?
