
# Indigenous AI Development Cycle

**Category:** Process & Methodology Technique
**Data Regime:** any
**Applicable Languages:** Endangered Indigenous languages with 1K–100K speakers where digital text tools are feasible but community trust must be built and maintained

## Description

An alternative AI development cycle designed specifically for Indigenous communities, contrasting sharply with the traditional "data crawl → model training → model usage" pipeline. The Indigenous AI Development Cycle (proposed by Pinhanez et al. 2024, based on field work 2022–2024 with Guarani Mbya and Nheengatu communities in Brazil) places community usage, community engagement, and community sovereignty at the structural center rather than as ethical add-ons.

**The traditional AI development cycle** (Figure 4a in the paper) is a linear pipeline: data crawling → model training → model usage → data collection → model retraining. This cycle starts with data extraction and measures success through model accuracy. Applied to Indigenous languages, this cycle is extractive by default: data is gathered from the Internet or community, processed centrally, and the community receives a deployed product with no structural role in the process.

**The Indigenous AI Development Cycle** (Figure 4b) reorganizes the same technical activities around two anchoring pillars:

- **Community Engagement** (top of cycle): all activities require ongoing participation and approval from community representatives.
- **Community Sovereignty** (bottom of cycle): the community retains legal and practical ownership of data and model artifacts.

The cycle has two time horizons, both anchored to **community usage**:

**Short-term sub-cycle:**
1. *Initialization*: build a starter model from publicly available linguistic data (dictionaries, lexicons, grammars, educational materials, folk tales). Obtain explicit community approval before initialization.
2. *Community usage*: deploy the starter model for community use as a technology probe — deliberately present an imperfect prototype to elicit feedback, not to impress.
3. *Data collection*: with direct community participation and under strict community supervision, collect new data generated by or with community members during tool use.
4. *Model training*: retrain the model on the new data; all decisions about training data inclusion are made with the community.

**Long-term sub-cycle:**
1. *Data governance*: establish structures and processes for long-term community ownership of data — who can access it, under what conditions, for how long.
2. *Model appropriation*: develop mechanisms for the community to take ownership of the model itself — hosting, maintenance, modification rights — independent of the research institution.

**Damage containment procedures:** Four mandatory practices during data handling:
1. Ensure all personnel understand ethical risks of releasing language data without authorization.
2. Do not release data or model weights publicly — not even to other AI researchers — without explicit community permission.
3. Deploy and test prototypes only with express community authorization.
4. Disclose all known limitations and shortcomings of tools transparently to communities and stakeholders.

## When to Use

- When starting any AI or NLP project that will involve collecting or using data from an Indigenous community.
- When transitioning a research project into a community-facing deployment (the cycle governs both initialization and community feedback loops).
- When writing research proposals, ethics board applications, or data governance agreements for Indigenous language technology work.
- When a community expresses concerns about data use — the cycle provides a structured vocabulary for negotiating control.

**Less suitable when:**
- The community is itself leading the technical development independently; defer to their process.
- Working exclusively with already-public, fully-consented archival data with no living community stake.

## How to Apply

**Step 0 — Pre-engagement assessment**

1. Identify the community's current digital infrastructure and Internet access situation. The Guarani Mbya community had only one year of high-speed Internet access when engagement began — technology assumptions must be calibrated.
2. Review the community's prior history with research institutions. Has previous NLP or AI work been extractive?
3. Identify all organizations working with the community (NGOs, government agencies, universities). Map who has legitimate authority to enter agreements.

**Step 1 — Initialization (starter model)**

4. Inventory all publicly available linguistic data: dictionaries, lexicons, theses, educational materials, folk tales, online content. Confirm all sources are ethically acceptable (avoid Bible data without explicit community consent).
5. Obtain community approval before building any model, even from public data. Present the initialization plan clearly — show what data sources will be used and what tools will be built.
6. Build the starter model bilingually (see Tiny-Data LLM Fine-Tuning). Accept that its quality will be limited; this is a technology probe, not a finished product.

**Step 2 — Community usage (technology probes)**

7. Deploy the prototype in the community context (school, community center, workshop setting). Frame the deployment explicitly as a technology probe — "we are showing you something incomplete to learn what you need."
8. Conduct usage workshops. Facilitate activities (e.g., writing exercises, group translation tasks) that generate authentic interaction with the tool. Observe how community members use or avoid it.
9. Explicitly downplay researcher competence ("show our incompetence") to reduce authority imbalance and invite genuine feedback rather than polite acceptance.
10. Document community responses: what tools were used, which were ignored, what was found helpful, what was frustrating.

**Step 3 — Data collection (community-supervised)**

11. All data collection requires prior community deliberation and approval. If the community requests suspension of data collection activities, comply immediately (as happened with the Tenondé-Porã community).
12. Design data collection activities that are intrinsically valuable to participants (writing workshops, documentation activities) — community members should benefit from participating, not just provide data.
13. Store all collected data locally under community control. Do not upload to cloud services without explicit authorization.

**Step 4 — Model training (participatory)**

14. Train updated models only on data explicitly approved by the community.
15. Present updated models to community members before deployment; conduct at least one demonstration session.
16. Report training decisions transparently: what data was used, what was excluded, why.

**Step 5 — Data governance and model appropriation (long-term)**

17. Draft a data governance agreement covering: who owns the data, who can access model weights, what happens to data when the research project ends, publication co-authorship protocols.
18. Plan for model appropriation: train community members to operate and update the model; document the pipeline so it can be maintained without the original research team.
19. If the community requests termination of the project, honor that request and ensure all data and model artifacts are returned or destroyed per the governance agreement.

## Pseudocode

```
procedure IndigenousAIDevelopmentCycle(community, researchers):

    // Step 0: Pre-engagement
    digital_context = assess_community_infrastructure(community)
    prior_history = review_research_history(community)
    authorized_bodies = identify_representative_organizations(community)

    // Initialization: build starter model from public linguistic data
    linguistic_data = inventory_public_sources(
        types=["dictionaries", "lexicons", "educational_materials", "folk_tales"])
    community_approval = seek_approval(authorized_bodies, plan=linguistic_data)
    if not community_approval:
        return "Revisit data plan with community"
    starter_model = fine_tune_bilingual(base_llm, linguistic_data)  // see Tiny-Data Fine-Tuning

    // SHORT-TERM CYCLE
    loop:
        // Community usage (technology probe)
        prototype = deploy_as_probe(starter_model, context="community_workshop")
        explicitly_present_limitations(prototype)
        usage_feedback = facilitate_writing_activities(prototype, community_members)
        
        // Data collection (community-supervised)
        collection_plan = co_design_with_community(usage_feedback)
        community_approval_data = seek_approval(authorized_bodies, plan=collection_plan)
        if not community_approval_data:
            suspend_and_await()
        new_data = collect_supervised(collection_plan, community_oversight=True)
        store_locally(new_data, access_control=community_governance_agreement)

        // Model retraining
        approved_data = filter_by_approval(new_data)
        updated_model = fine_tune_bilingual(starter_model, approved_data)
        demonstrate_to_community(updated_model)
        starter_model = updated_model

        if community_requests_suspension():
            suspend_immediately()
            return

    // LONG-TERM SUB-CYCLE (parallel to short-term)
    governance = draft_data_governance_agreement(
        data_ownership=community,
        model_weight_access=community_controlled,
        project_end_procedures=defined,
        publication_protocols=co_authorship)
    train_community_operators(model=starter_model, documentation=full_pipeline)
    // Model appropriation: community can maintain/update independently
```

## Evidence

Pinhanez et al. (2024) report on two field engagements implementing this cycle, 2022–2024:

**Guarani Mbya (Tenondé-Porã community, ~1,500 people, São Paulo outskirts):**
- 14 two-hour workshops over three months at the Gwyra Pepo Indigenous High School, with high school students.
- Technology probes (rudimentary translator + word/sentence completion) used in writing activities.
- Community impact: students began producing longer, more complex sentences over the workshop series; community entered a broader discussion about digital access and appropriate technology use.
- Process outcome: community requested suspension of activities while deliberating on continuation; research team complied, confirmed no language data had been collected, and suspended the Guarani Mbya writing assistant development.
- Key finding: showing researcher incompetence ("we know we built something bad") was effective at reducing authority imbalance and enabling genuine feedback.

**Nheengatu (Amazonian community, ~20,000 speakers):**
- Partnership begun after Guarani Mbya experience; focused on two workstreams: (1) writing assistant for Nheengatu speakers at the State University of Campinas (Baré ethnicity students); (2) translation support for professional Nheengatu translators.
- Nheengatu Clean dataset (6,621 pairs): human evaluation showed 48% "near perfect" outputs after data cleaning — a quality level that enabled meaningful writing support.
- Tools deployed: Nheengatu writing assistant with Portuguese-to-Nheengatu translation, word completion, and next-word completion (Figures 12–13 in paper).
- Ongoing engagement: collaborating with a team of professional Nheengatu translators who recently completed the Brazilian Constitution translation into Nheengatu.

**Contrast with traditional cycle:** Internet data crawling for endangered Indigenous languages is largely infeasible — half of Brazilian Indigenous languages with ≤10 speakers have no Wikipedia entries (Vasconcelos et al. 2024). The traditional crawl-first cycle is structurally inappropriate for these communities.

## Variations & Configuration

- **Rapid initialization:** When community timelines are short, compress initialization to use only dictionaries and lexicons (the most ethically clean sources) without folk tales or theses. Accept lower starter model quality and rely on community usage feedback to guide what data to add.
- **Multi-community deployment:** Build the writing assistant platform as a "digital shell" (see Cyclical Engagement Process) — a parameterized template where language-specific data, lexicons, and model weights can be swapped. Demonstrated feasibility across Guarani Mbya and Nheengatu in the same platform.
- **Youth-focused deployment:** Target teenagers and young adults specifically, since they are the generation most at risk of language attrition. Design tools for digital writing contexts they already use (messaging apps, social media). The Guarani Mbya students used their language as a private code in Free Fire gaming — this behavior is a data point about what digital uses feel natural.
- **Professional translator partnership:** For languages with professional translators (e.g., Nheengatu), integrate the AI tool as a productivity aid for translators rather than as a community-facing end-user tool. This provides higher-quality feedback and more direct benefit from improved translation accuracy.

## Strengths & Weaknesses

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- |
| Structurally centers community sovereignty — not as an add-on but as a pillar of the cycle | Significantly slower than the traditional data-crawl cycle; community deliberation takes time that grant timelines often cannot accommodate |
| Technology probe approach surfaces authentic feedback that user research and linguistic analysis alone would miss | Community suspension (as happened with Guarani Mbya) can interrupt technical development indefinitely — requires genuine acceptance of this risk |
| Short-term and long-term sub-cycles address both immediate tool utility and long-term data governance | Model appropriation (Step 5) requires sustained training of community members — not achievable in a single project grant |
| Damage containment procedures provide concrete operational guidance for researchers | Contamination risk from Bible data persists even after governance agreements — requires active monitoring at every deployment |
| Bilingual over multilingual finding challenges a common NLP assumption; reduces training complexity | Applicable primarily to languages with 1K–100K speakers and some existing text; does not address speech-primary languages or those with &lt;100 speakers |
| Replicability via ILM framework: same training code, different language data | Youth adoption of writing tools is uncertain; workshops showed some engagement but tools were not yet integrated into daily digital life |
