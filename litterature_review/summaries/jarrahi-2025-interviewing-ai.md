# Interviewing AI: Using qualitative methods to explore and capture machines' characteristics and behaviors

**Authors:** Mohammad Hossein Jarrahi
**Year:** 2025
**Venue:** Big Data & Society, July–September 2025, pages 1–15 (Sage). DOI: 10.1177/20539517251381697

---

## Core Argument

AI systems — especially LLMs — exhibit emergent, non-deterministic, and context-dependent behaviours that quantitative benchmarks alone cannot capture. The "interviewing AI" framework applies qualitative research traditions (ethnography, probing, discourse analysis, triangulation) directly to the study of AI systems, treating them as subjects of inquiry rather than merely engineering artefacts, in order to surface the situated, adaptive, and ideologically laden nature of their behaviours.

## Key Concepts

- **Machine behavior:** A nascent research field that studies AI systems as agentic entities with distinct behavioural patterns, rather than as purely deterministic tools (following Rahwan et al., 2019).
- **Exploratory familiarization:** First-hand, open-ended experimentation with an AI system to develop intuition about its capabilities, blind spots, and idiosyncrasies before structured investigation.
- **Systematic probing:** Purposive, structured prompting to elicit and document specific behaviours — including hallucinations, boundary failures, and internal reasoning — using approaches such as systematic prompt variation, role-playing, boundary testing, chain-of-thought, incremental, least-to-most, and counterfactual prompting.
- **Temporal Interaction Analysis (TIA):** Longitudinal, diachronic study of how AI behaviour evolves over time or repeated interactions (including autoethnography).
- **Comparative Synchronic Analysis (CSA):** Snapshot comparison of multiple AI systems responding to identical prompts at a single point in time.
- **Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA):** Applied to AI outputs to surface hegemonic ideologies, social biases, and power dynamics embedded in training data or design choices.
- **Triangulation:** Integration of qualitative "interviewing AI" with public interaction datasets, human/expert studies, and quantitative methods to build a multi-dimensional understanding.

## Main Findings

- Qualitative methods reveal patterns that aggregate benchmarks miss: affordances, breakdowns, sociopolitical entanglements, and implicit biases embedded in training data or surfacing through situated interactions.
- Probing can serve three distinct goals: (1) documenting invisible/emergent behaviours, (2) revealing boundaries through breakdown analysis, and (3) unpacking internal reasoning and logic — each achievable through different prompting strategies.
- TIA and CSA are complementary: TIA captures diachronic change (critical given that models are updated post-deployment); CSA enables rapid cross-system comparison.
- Human-AI exchanges should be treated as unified analytical units, since meaning is co-produced in the interaction; the researcher's prompting strategies actively shape AI behaviour and must be reflexively examined.
- Ethical conduct requires: avoiding over-anthropomorphisation, transparent documentation (audit trails), clear articulation of method limits, and reflexive analysis of the researcher's own positionality and bias.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

The interviewing AI framework offers a methodological toolkit directly applicable to the Mila Indigenous AI project. When evaluating how LLMs represent, misrepresent, or fail to represent Mohawk language and Six Nations cultural knowledge, CDA-based probing (e.g., asking models to describe Mohawk culture, generate Mohawk text, or discuss Indigenous concepts) can surface bias patterns that accuracy metrics miss. Role-playing scenarios (asking the model to take on the perspective of a Mohawk speaker or language teacher) may reveal implicit assumptions. TIA is particularly relevant for tracking how model behaviour changes across versions. The emphasis on researcher reflexivity and transparent documentation aligns with the ethical obligations of community-engaged Indigenous research.

## Limitations & Critiques

- The framework is presented as a normative conceptual map rather than the result of a single unified empirical study; no complete case study integrates all six elements simultaneously.
- The paper focuses exclusively on LLM-based chatbots accessed through prompting interfaces; it explicitly notes that the framework may not generalise to other AI system types.
- The ethical discussion, while substantive, does not address the specific obligations that arise when AI systems are studied in relation to Indigenous peoples and their cultural heritage — a significant gap for this research context.

## Questions & Follow-ups

- How can the "interviewing AI" probing protocol be adapted to assess Mohawk language model outputs with Six Nations community members as co-evaluators, rather than relying solely on researcher judgment?
- What audit trail practices are appropriate when documenting AI-community interactions involving sensitive cultural knowledge that communities may not wish to make public?
