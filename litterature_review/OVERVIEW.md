# Literature Review — Synthesis

> Last updated: 2026-05-20. 26 papers reviewed.

## Scope

This review synthesizes 26 papers relevant to the development of generative AI with and for Indigenous communities, with a current focus on the Mohawk language (Kanien'kéha) and the Six Nations community. Papers were selected for their bearing on four intersecting concerns: epistemic justice and representational harms produced by large language models; NLP tools and evaluation frameworks for low-resource and endangered languages (including polysynthetic and oral-tradition languages); ethical and decolonial methodologies for community co-construction; and the evaluation and benchmarking of language models on culturally specific tasks. The review serves the IndigenousAI project at Mila (Montréal Institute of Learning Algorithms), which seeks to develop AI tools that support Mohawk language revitalization in genuine partnership with the Six Nations community.

## Thematic Synthesis

### Epistemic Justice & Representation

The literature reviewed here converged on a central finding: large language models encode and reproduce epistemic hierarchies that systematically disadvantage non-Western, non-English, and especially Indigenous communities. Kay et al. (2024) offered the most theoretically developed account of this dynamic, introducing the concept of "generative algorithmic epistemic injustice" — a distinct form of harm that goes beyond classification-based bias. Their taxonomy identified four modes: amplified testimonial injustice (models propagate social prejudices at mass scale), manipulative testimonial injustice (deliberate AI-assisted fabrication targeting marginalized groups), hermeneutical ignorance (models lack the embodied cultural depth to represent certain experiences faithfully), and hermeneutical access injustice (qualitatively inferior model outputs for speakers of under-resourced languages). The last two modes are structurally embedded in current architectures — they are not incidental failures but consequences of how training data is assembled and how alignment is performed.

Barman et al. (2025) traced one of the primary mechanisms of this harm to the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipeline. The initial evaluator pool for models such as InstructGPT was drawn from a narrow demographic (predominantly young, university-educated, Southeast Asian and American workers on crowdsourcing platforms), with no Indigenous evaluators represented in disclosed demographics. Drawing on social epistemology, they argued that this structural exclusion produces sycophancy — models learn to please the evaluators who trained them, amplifying those evaluators' cultural assumptions — and that remediation requires not only diversifying evaluators but reorganizing feedback aggregation methods so that minority viewpoints are preserved rather than averaged out. Their analysis of positionality (following Harding) directly supported the epistemic claim that Indigenous community members possess a form of knowledge about AI systems affecting their communities that outside researchers cannot substitute for.

Adilazuarda et al. (2024) surveyed more than ninety studies of culture in LLMs and found that none explicitly defined culture; all relied on proxies — most commonly country of origin or language — that rendered Indigenous nations, whose cultural territories do not align with state borders, effectively invisible. Their distinction between "thin" and "thick" description (following Geertz) named a further dimension of the representational deficit: LLMs tend to produce outsider-view, thin accounts of cultural practices, lacking the interpretive context that community members themselves supply. Pawar et al. (2025) and Liu et al. (2025), surveying the broader landscape of culturally aware NLP, independently confirmed the skew: Africa, Central Asia, South/Southeast Asia, and Indigenous cultures worldwide were severely underrepresented in both training data and evaluation benchmarks, while Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) cultures dominated. Singh et al. (2025) made the WEIRD bias concrete and measurable: 86.5% of the culturally sensitive questions in the MMLU benchmark — the de facto standard for LLM evaluation — reflected Western culture, and 64.5% required North American regional knowledge specifically.

Khanuja et al. (2026) examined the representational deficit from a mechanistic angle. Using sparse autoencoders to probe internal model representations, they showed that LLMs encode culture-specific knowledge in linearly decodable internal features and that under implicit (unspecified) prompting, 60% of Gemma-2-9B's cultural defaults aligned with the United States or United Kingdom. This "Anglophone default" was not simply an artifact of language choice but reflected the concentration of training data from particular cultural contexts. For cultures with sparse training-data presence — the situation of any Indigenous language community — the model's latent cultural knowledge was either absent or unelicitable by standard prompting, even when some representation existed. This finding suggested that representational failure for Indigenous communities is twofold: a data absence problem and an elicitation failure problem, with different remedies for each.

### Language Revitalization & NLP

The NLP literature on low-resource and endangered Indigenous languages, though smaller than the broader cultural AI literature, produced a set of technically grounded findings with direct implications for Mohawk language tool development. Bird (2020) provided the foundational critical analysis, arguing that the speech and language technology field had reproduced colonial logics — treating Indigenous languages as data to be extracted, problems to be solved, or codes to be modernized — and had accumulated little evidence, after twenty-five years of documentation projects, that technology had meaningfully reversed language shift. His critique targeted the "gold data ideology" (preoccupation with clean, annotated corpora), the "zero-resource" framing (which erased existing community knowledge), and the "language documentation mechanism" (which positioned linguists as central agents and speakers as data sources). As an alternative, he proposed reorienting evaluation away from system performance metrics toward measures of community capability expansion.

Zhang et al. (2022), working with the Cherokee language, operationalized this reorientation into a three-stage roadmap — community relationship building first, NLP-assisted education second, language-specific NLP research third — and demonstrated that community members had expressed specific, concrete technology priorities (machine translation for drafting and editing, OCR for digitizing manuscripts, ASR for audio transcription) that differed from what the NLP community had been producing. Their technical findings on Cherokee were directly relevant to Mohawk: Cherokee's polysynthetic morphology (where a single word encodes clause-level meaning through complex affixation) posed particular challenges for standard subword tokenization, and they found that character-level approaches in transliterated script performed better than byte-pair encoding for MT. Liu et al. (2022), working with four Indigenous communities including Cayuga (a closely related Haudenosaunee language), extended the ethical dimension: they distinguished endangered languages from other low-resource languages on the grounds that data cannot simply be generated on demand, and that the transcription bottleneck, the irreplaceability of Master Speakers (fluent elders who have accepted apprentices through oral tradition), and the historical trauma of linguicide created constraints that standard NLP assumptions violated.

The AmericasNLP shared tasks tracked progress on machine translation for Indigenous languages of the Americas over multiple iterations. Ebrahimi et al. (2023) reported that despite average ChrF improvements of nearly ten points since 2021, human evaluation of outputs for Bribri, Chatino, and Otomí showed that near-perfect translations remained extremely rare and that Otomí outputs were rated 1 out of 5 on both fluency and meaning for the majority of evaluated sentences. De Gibert et al. (2025) observed a performance plateau: the NLLB-1.3B multilingual fine-tuned baseline was extremely difficult to surpass, and new educational-materials generation and metric-development subtasks revealed that rule-based and knowledge-grounded approaches consistently outperformed pure LLM prompting for morphologically complex, low-resource languages. Nahuatl (Western Sierra Puebla variety), the most morphologically demanding language in the 2025 tasks, achieved only 17.5% exact-match accuracy under the best submitted system — a figure that communicated starkly the gap between current NLP capabilities and anything practically useful for learners.

Rai and Pal (2025) demonstrated that SentencePiece subword tokenization substantially reduced the out-of-vocabulary problem for Amharic — a morphologically rich Semitic language — producing a 126% relative BLEU improvement over an RNN baseline. While Amharic's morphological richness belongs to a different typological family than Mohawk's polysynthesis, the core tokenization challenge (standard word-level tokens cannot handle productive morphological paradigms) was shared. Ansari et al. (2026), working on classical Persian poetry, showed that adding audio recitation features to a text-only NMT system raised BLEU from 9.85 to 17.95 and substantially increased Culture-Specific Item recall — a finding relevant to Indigenous language contexts where oral tradition is primary and audio archives exist but are underused. Across these technical papers, a consistent picture emerged: existing multilingual models (NLLB, mBART) offered a productive starting point through multilingual fine-tuning, but morphological complexity and data scarcity imposed a ceiling that no current approach reliably cleared, particularly for polysynthetic languages.

The finding that "language does not equal culture" emerged from multiple directions. Zhang et al. (2025), using the CultureScope benchmark, showed that PolyLM — a model with additional multilingual pretraining — performed significantly worse than same-sized monolingual models on cultural tasks, demonstrating that more tokens in a language did not yield more cultural knowledge. Feng et al. (2025) similarly found that standard fine-tuning on culturally specific data produced cross-language inconsistency, where models answered cultural questions correctly in the local language but failed in English. Monazzah et al. (2025), testing models on Persian cultural stories, found that translating cultural content into English degraded performance by six to fifteen percentage points. These results collectively warned against treating Mohawk-language pretraining as a proxy for cultural competence, and against relying on English-mediated evaluation of Mohawk cultural outputs.

### Methodology & Co-construction

The reviewed literature produced a convergent normative framework for ethical research with Indigenous and marginalized communities, though it drew on different traditions and operationalized the principles at different levels of concreteness. Bird (2020) provided the most comprehensive critique and the most operational alternative: building genuine relationships before any data collection or tool development, identifying community goals from the ground up, treating community knowledge holders as authorities rather than data sources, and measuring success by local capability expansion rather than system performance. He drew on "yarning" — an Indigenous Australian relational research method — as a model for how researchers should enter community settings. Liu et al. (2022) concretized this into six actionable recommendations: build community bonds first; offer co-authorship to community contributors; document data collection challenges transparently; clarify data ownership including copyright for Master Speakers; create technology jointly; and plan for how technology output integrates into existing revitalization work.

Cooper et al. (2024), drawing on interviews with seventeen researchers working with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, proposed a cyclical engagement process: seek community needs, engage representative bodies, negotiate control, create community benefit, store data accessibly, and scale tools as "digital shells" adaptable by other communities. A finding that cut across all three of these foundational methodology papers was that community members universally prioritized cultural transmission across generations as the goal of language technology — not linguistic documentation, digital preservation, or benchmark performance. This goal orientation had direct methodological consequences: communities wanted technology that enabled learning between humans, not technology that substituted for human transmission.

Zhao et al. (2026), working with Hawaiian educators, demonstrated that co-design workshops with Indigenous community members produced qualitatively different requirements for AI auditing tools than top-down researcher frameworks had anticipated. Hawaiian educators' concept of knowledge genealogy (mo'opuna — tracing not just a source's author but who taught that author) was incommensurable with standard factual verification paradigms, yet it was the relevant epistemic standard for assessing cultural trustworthiness in their community. The paper also demonstrated that cultural expertise was unevenly distributed within the community: Kanaka Maoli (Native Hawaiian) teachers recognized representational harms that recently relocated mainland teachers did not, requiring tiered auditing roles rather than treating all users as equivalent auditors. This finding challenged the implicit egalitarianism of many participatory design frameworks and reinforced the authority of specific community knowledge holders.

Qadri et al. (2025) provided the theoretical framework for moving evaluation beyond quantitative benchmarks to what they called "thick evaluations," explicitly adapting Ryle's and Geertz's anthropological concept of thick description. Their participatory workshops in South Asia found that participants across three sites disagreed on what "correct" cultural representation looked like even within a single country — demonstrating that culture was contested and dynamic, not a stable ground truth to be measured. The five dimensions they identified for cultural representation (incorrectness, missingness, specificity, coherence, connotation) offered a vocabulary for evaluation that foregrounded what communities found harmful rather than what researchers found measurable. Jarrahi (2025) extended the methodological toolkit in a complementary direction: applying qualitative research traditions (critical discourse analysis, systematic probing, longitudinal temporal interaction analysis) to AI systems as subjects of inquiry, enabling researchers to surface bias patterns that aggregate benchmarks systematically missed.

### Evaluation & Benchmarking

The evaluation literature revealed a field whose measurement frameworks were falling behind its technical ambitions. Adilazuarda et al. (2024) documented that the dominant evaluation method for cultural competence in LLMs — black-box probing with culturally conditioned prompts — suffered from profound prompt sensitivity: results might reflect how questions were phrased rather than genuine cultural knowledge or alignment. Khan et al. (2025) provided the most systematic empirical demonstration of this instability: across established cultural surveys (Hofstede's Value Survey Module, the Global Values Survey), LLM cultural preferences shifted by more than one standard deviation of real between-country human variation when only presentation format was changed (question direction, Likert scale size, identifier type). More damning still, under both instruction-based and optimized prompting, LLM responses clustered by model architecture rather than by target country — human responses from other cultures were better proxies for each other's preferences than LLMs prompted to represent a specific culture. These findings constituted a methodological warning: survey-based cultural alignment evaluation produced artifacts of design, not stable properties of models.

Branco et al. (2026) identified three structural flaws in existing cultural alignment benchmarks beyond instability: reliance on externally imposed, stereotyped ("touristic cliché") knowledge rather than insider community perspectives; focus on encyclopedic facts widely available in training data, yielding low discriminative power between fine-tuned and base models; and the "oracle effect," where explicit cultural identifiers embedded in question text inflated scores by up to forty-two percentage points. Their proposed guidelines required questions written from endogenous community perspectives, answerable only from genuine insider knowledge, and capable of stumping general-purpose models. Empirically, their community-insider benchmark (Tuguesice-PT) discriminated between a culturally fine-tuned model and its base by 14.07 percentage points, compared to only 1.72 points on the mainstream benchmark. Sakhaeirad et al. (2026) revealed a related failure mode through the DIVANBENCH benchmark for Persian: models exhibited severe acquiescence bias, accepting culturally appropriate statements at 84–92% accuracy while rejecting clear violations at only 19–48%, producing a 21-point factual-to-conceptual performance gap that worsened — not improved — with monolingual pretraining.

Bhagat et al. (2026), through large-scale annotation with 108 cultural experts across 71 regions and 14 languages, found that 88% of LLM-generated stories contained at least one cultural misrepresentation, averaging 5.42 per story. Critically, models that scored 77% on cultural knowledge questions in English still misrepresented culture in 88% of generated stories — a "knowledge-generation gap" demonstrating that possessing cultural knowledge did not guarantee its faithful application in open-ended generation. Misrepresentations increased 56% for mid-resource languages and quadrupled for low-resource languages, and stories about peri-urban, lesser-known localities showed significantly more errors than those about major cities. Monazzah et al. (2025) reached a parallel conclusion through story-driven evaluation of Persian cultural knowledge: models relied on surface-level lexical cues rather than synthesizing narrative context, and Persian-specific fine-tuned models underperformed their base models — a warning that language fine-tuning did not transfer cultural understanding.

Several papers proposed new evaluation approaches that addressed these deficiencies, though each had its own limitations. Ansari et al. (2026) introduced Culture-Specific Item (CSI) recall — a targeted metric measuring what proportion of culturally significant lexical items in a source were correctly rendered in translation output — as a complement to standard MT metrics. Sadr et al. (2025) demonstrated that role-play scenario evaluation, using structured situation-role-context-utterance-expected-response tuples for the Persian practice of taarof, captured cultural pragmatic competence that standard politeness classifiers systematically missed (84.5% of model outputs rated "polite" by classifier but only 41.7% culturally appropriate). Feng et al. (2025) proposed fine-grained reward metrics (cultural precision and recall over atomic knowledge units) for training and evaluating cultural alignment. Qadri et al. (2025) argued that all quantitative approaches remained "thin" and that authentic evaluation required participatory workshops where communities themselves determined what counted as misrepresentation. This plurality of proposed solutions — none yet comprehensive — indicated that the field lacked an agreed-upon evaluation paradigm for cultural competence, particularly for communities underrepresented in training data.

## Convergences

- **WEIRD bias is pervasive and measurable across evaluation frameworks.** Multiple papers independently documented that LLMs and their evaluation benchmarks disproportionately represent Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic cultures, to the systematic exclusion of Indigenous, African, and other non-WEIRD communities (Adilazuarda et al., 2024; Pawar et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2025; Khan et al., 2025).

- **Cultural knowledge in LLMs does not straightforwardly translate into cultural competence in generation.** The knowledge-generation gap (Bhagat et al., 2026), the factual-conceptual gap (Sakhaeirad et al., 2026), and the surface-vs.-deep politeness discrepancy (Sadr et al., 2025) all established independently that factual retrieval accuracy was a poor predictor of culturally appropriate generative behavior, undermining benchmark-only evaluation regimes.

- **Community co-construction and process quality are prerequisites, not optional additions.** Bird (2020), Cooper et al. (2024), Liu et al. (2022), Zhang et al. (2022), and Zhao et al. (2026) all concluded, from different empirical bases, that the ethical and practical quality of language technology for marginalized communities depended more on engagement process than on technical outputs — and that process failures reproduced colonial harms regardless of system performance.

- **Standard NLP metrics are insufficient for evaluating culturally and linguistically complex outputs.** ChrF over BLEU for morphologically rich languages (Ebrahimi et al., 2023; de Gibert et al., 2025), CSI-recall over BLEU for cultural fidelity (Ansari et al., 2026), scenario-based over politeness-classifier evaluation (Sadr et al., 2025), and thick over thin evaluation (Qadri et al., 2025) all pointed to the same conclusion: existing automatic metrics systematically undercount culturally relevant failures.

- **Polysynthetic morphology poses distinct challenges for standard NLP pipelines.** Zhang et al. (2022), Ebrahimi et al. (2023), de Gibert et al. (2025), and Rai and Pal (2025) collectively established that standard subword tokenization (BPE) failed on morphologically complex languages, that Nahuatl and Cherokee — languages typologically similar to Mohawk — showed the worst performance in shared tasks, and that rule-based or morphological-grounding approaches often outperformed pure LLM prompting for these languages.

- **Representational gaps compound for smaller localities and lower-resource communities.** Bhagat et al. (2026), Singh et al. (2025), Ebrahimi et al. (2023), and Khanuja et al. (2026) all found that performance degraded significantly for communities, regions, and languages with smaller training-data footprints — a pattern that implies the situation for Indigenous languages will be worse, not merely analogous, relative to what is observed for mid-resource settings.

- **Data sovereignty and community control over language resources are recognized as necessary conditions for ethical AI development.** Kay et al. (2024), Cooper et al. (2024), Liu et al. (2022), Zhao et al. (2026), and Liu et al. (2025) all explicitly named data sovereignty — communities' rights to own and govern data derived from their languages and cultures — as a prerequisite for ethical Indigenous language AI, citing frameworks such as CARE, OCAP, and Kukutai and Taylor's Indigenous data sovereignty agenda.

## Tensions & Divergences

- **Co-construction as principle vs. co-construction as practice.** Bird (2020), Cooper et al. (2024), and Liu et al. (2022) all advocated for genuine community co-design, but their specific protocols, power structures, and community authority frameworks differed. Cooper et al. (2024) foregrounded CARE principles and negotiated data control; Liu et al. (2022) emphasized co-authorship and data copyright for Master Speakers; Bird (2020) focused on capability expansion and oral-tradition primacy. These frameworks are complementary in intent but potentially divergent in practice — particularly regarding which community members hold authority, how disagreements within communities are handled, and what counts as adequate reciprocity.

- **Whether increasing language-specific training data helps or hurts.** Sakhaeirad et al. (2026) found that continued Persian-language pretraining degraded rejection accuracy by forty-three percentage points (the "pretraining paradox"). Feng et al. (2025) demonstrated gains from target-aware multilingual critique fine-tuning. Pawar et al. (2025) reviewed evidence that training-based alignment outperformed prompting-based approaches. Zhang et al. (2025) showed that additional multilingual pretraining did not improve cultural knowledge. These results are difficult to reconcile and may reflect differences in evaluation design, model size, and the specific type of cultural knowledge being assessed — but they collectively caution against assuming that scaling language-specific data will improve cultural competence.

- **Quantitative benchmarking vs. thick participatory evaluation.** Khan et al. (2025) argued that survey-based cultural alignment evaluation was fundamentally unreliable, while papers such as Bhagat et al. (2026), Monazzah et al. (2025), and Ansari et al. (2026) invested substantial effort in building new quantitative benchmarks. Qadri et al. (2025) positioned thick participatory evaluation as a necessary alternative to any quantitative approach, while Singh et al. (2025) and Branco et al. (2026) argued that better-designed quantitative benchmarks were the right response. The field did not resolve whether the problem was with quantitative evaluation per se or with the specific quantitative approaches deployed so far.

- **The role of fine-tuning for cultural adaptation.** Sadr et al. (2025) demonstrated that DPO fine-tuning on a small set of 450 taarof scenarios produced a 42.3-point accuracy gain, approaching native speaker performance. Monazzah et al. (2025) found that Persian-fine-tuned models underperformed their base models on cultural tasks. Feng et al. (2025) showed gains from critique-driven fine-tuning but only for well-resourced cultural contexts. This divergence may reflect differences in the specificity of the target cultural knowledge (a single pragmatic norm vs. broad cultural knowledge), the quality of the fine-tuning signal, and whether evaluation was in-distribution or generalizing — but no paper resolved the conditions under which fine-tuning reliably improves cultural competence.

- **Whether technology fundamentally serves or threatens Indigenous language revitalization.** Bird (2020) argued that documentation technology had not produced evidence of reversing language shift and that its extractive logic actively risked re-enacting colonial harm. Liu et al. (2022) and Zhang et al. (2022) were more optimistic, showing communities that did express specific technology needs (ASR, MT, OCR) and identifying principled workflows for meeting them. This divergence was not primarily empirical but reflected different framings of the purpose of language technology — whether revitalization is about capability expansion for communities or about producing linguistic resources for researchers.

- **How to handle restricted cultural knowledge in AI systems.** Branco et al. (2026) proposed that cultural benchmarks should include only knowledge that community insiders would share without concern — but gave no guidance on how to identify that boundary or enforce it technically. Zhao et al. (2026) explicitly raised data sovereignty as requiring that flagged examples from auditing remain under community control. Kay et al. (2024) noted that participatory methods and data sovereignty remained insufficient in practice. No paper provided an operational framework for how AI systems should handle cultural knowledge that communities have determined should not be encoded, reproduced, or made publicly queryable.

## Gaps & Open Questions

- **Indigenous languages, including Mohawk, are entirely absent from the evaluation benchmarks reviewed.** No paper in this set included an Indigenous language from the Americas in a cultural alignment benchmark, and the AmericasNLP shared tasks treated performance on MT as the primary metric without engaging with cultural representation evaluation. The construction of community-grounded cultural evaluation resources for Kanien'kéha, informed by Six Nations knowledge holders, is identified as a need but not addressed anywhere in the reviewed literature.

- **Polysynthetic language challenges for generative AI remain underexplored.** While Zhang et al. (2022), Ebrahimi et al. (2023), and de Gibert et al. (2025) documented the failure of standard tokenization and prompting for polysynthetic morphology, no paper proposed a theoretically grounded alternative architecture or training paradigm specifically for polysynthetic language generation — as opposed to classification or translation tasks. The implications of polysynthesis for LLM pretraining, alignment, and evaluation remained open.

- **The "acquiescence bias" and factual-conceptual gap have only been studied for Persian and Korean.** Sakhaeirad et al. (2026) documented the catastrophic failure of models to reject cultural norm violations (acquiescence bias) and the 21-point factual-to-conceptual gap, and proposed these as general phenomena — but both papers cited a single non-WEIRD language. Whether these failure modes manifest differently for languages with very sparse training-data presence (such as Mohawk, where even cultural facts are largely absent from pretraining corpora) was not investigated.

- **The relationship between oral tradition and AI system design is theoretically underdeveloped.** Bird (2020) and Liu et al. (2022) both emphasized the primacy of oral tradition in Indigenous language revitalization, and Ansari et al. (2026) demonstrated gains from incorporating audio modality in NMT. But no paper investigated how AI systems could be designed to support oral-first learning paradigms — where the target output is not text but spoken-language competence acquired through human relationships — rather than merely processing transcriptions of oral content.

- **Community-controlled fine-tuning and alignment pipelines do not yet exist for endangered-language settings.** Barman et al. (2025) proposed making RLHF more pluralistic, Sadr et al. (2025) demonstrated that DPO on small datasets could close large cultural competence gaps, and Feng et al. (2025) showed critique-driven fine-tuning gains — but all of these approaches assumed access to existing datasets or LLM infrastructure that communities could neither own nor audit. A pipeline for community-controlled alignment, where Indigenous community members define the reward signal and retain ownership of training data, did not exist in the reviewed literature and was identified as a need by multiple papers without being developed.

- **Evaluation of "missingness" — what the model cannot say — remains methodologically underdeveloped.** Qadri et al. (2025) identified missingness (important cultural elements absent from outputs) as one of the five dimensions of cultural misrepresentation most important to community members, and noted that standard benchmarks were entirely blind to it (you cannot measure what is not there). For endangered languages undergoing revitalization, missingness may be the dominant failure mode: a model generating Mohawk language content may fail not by producing wrong forms but by being unable to produce culturally significant forms at all. No reviewed paper offered a methodology for systematically evaluating what a model cannot produce.

- **The intersection of gender, kinship, and clan structures in Indigenous language AI remains unaddressed.** Sadr et al. (2025) found that models exhibited gender biases in generating culturally appropriate responses, likely encoding cultural gender stereotypes rather than situationally appropriate norms. In Haudenosaunee communities, gender, clan, and kinship relationships are structurally encoded in language (through verb morphology, forms of address, and social obligations) and carry political significance distinct from Western gender categories. No paper in the reviewed set examined how colonial-era gender and kinship frameworks embedded in LLM training data would interact with Indigenous linguistic and social structures, or how to detect and mitigate such harms.
