# Epistemic Injustice in Generative AI

**Authors:** Jackie Kay, Atoosa Kasirzadeh, Shakir Mohamed
**Year:** 2024
**Venue:** arXiv preprint (arXiv:2408.11441v1), submitted August 2024
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11441

---

## Core Argument

Generative AI systems constitute a new and distinct form of epistemic injustice — "generative algorithmic epistemic injustice" — that goes beyond prior work on classification-based algorithmic bias. The paper introduces a four-dimensional taxonomy of these injustices (amplified testimonial, manipulative testimonial, hermeneutical ignorance, hermeneutical access) and argues that, without principled intervention, generative AI will systematically undermine the credibility and epistemic resources of marginalized groups at unprecedented scale. Conversely, these same systems can be re-engineered as tools of epistemic resistance.

## Key Concepts

- **Testimonial injustice:** Unfair discrediting of a speaker's account due to identity-based prejudice; in the generative AI context, the AI is granted excess credibility over the marginalized individual.
- **Hermeneutical injustice:** Gaps in shared interpretive resources that make it impossible to articulate or understand certain lived experiences; generative AI perpetuates this by lacking embodied cultural knowledge.
- **Amplified testimonial injustice:** Generative AI magnifies existing social biases from training data, amplifying misinformation about marginalized groups at mass scale.
- **Manipulative testimonial injustice:** Humans intentionally steer AI to fabricate false narratives — deepfakes, propaganda — that discredit marginalized groups.
- **Hermeneutical ignorance:** The AI erases or misportrays marginalized experiences (e.g., imposing the "American smile" on historical non-Western figures) because it lacks cultural depth.
- **Hermeneutical access injustice:** Unequal AI quality across languages and geographies denies certain communities access to accurate information.
- **Epistemic ecosystem:** The shared collective body of knowledge that generative AI can pollute through false narratives and representational harms.
- **Data sovereignty:** Community-controlled ownership and governance of data (Kukutai & Taylor 2016), proposed as a remedy for representational harm.

## Main Findings

- LLMs memorize and reproduce social biases at scale; GPT-4 propagated false narratives in 100% of tested "misinformation fingerprint" prompts (2023 NewsGuard study).
- Generative AI lacks the embodied, culturally situated knowledge that humans acquire through lived experience, making hermeneutical ignorance structurally embedded in current architectures.
- Multilingual access injustice is documented: GPT-3.5 reported different civilian casualty counts for the same airstrikes depending on the query language (Turkish vs. Kurdish, Hebrew vs. Arabic).
- Participatory development methods and data sovereignty are necessary but currently insufficient conditions for epistemic justice — they have gained little real foothold in industry.
- Generative AI can be redirected as a tool for epistemic justice: identifying testimonial injustices at scale via red-teaming and embedding detection, and generating hermeneutical resources that surface marginalized cultural narratives.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

This paper provides the philosophical grounding for why an Indigenous AI project must treat epistemic justice — not just technical performance — as a core design criterion. The concept of hermeneutical ignorance directly names what happens when an LLM trained on English-dominant corpora attempts to engage with Mohawk language or Six Nations cultural knowledge: it lacks the interpretive resources to represent those experiences faithfully. The paper's emphasis on data sovereignty (Kukutai & Taylor) and participatory design maps directly onto Indigenous community co-construction principles. The multilingual access injustice section is particularly relevant to endangered language contexts, where "under-resourced" languages receive qualitatively inferior model outputs.

## Limitations & Critiques

- The paper draws primarily on Fricker's framework while acknowledging (but not fully integrating) Black feminist precursors such as Patricia Hill Collins — the authors note this debt but do not restructure the argument around it.
- Proposed interventions (watermarking, red-teaming, participatory methods) remain largely at the level of principles; the paper does not engage deeply with the practical barriers to community-led AI development, especially for small or endangered language communities.
- The case studies are drawn from high-profile Western contexts (Uyghur internment, Parkland, US elections); examples from Indigenous communities are absent, leaving the theory's application to those contexts implicit rather than demonstrated.

## Questions & Follow-ups

- How do the four dimensions of generative epistemic injustice apply specifically to Indigenous language AI, where the community may have deliberately restricted access to certain knowledge as an act of cultural sovereignty?
- Explore Kukutai & Taylor (2016) "Indigenous Data Sovereignty: Toward an Agenda" — directly cited here and foundational to the Indigenous data governance literature.
- How can hermeneutical resource generation (the paper's constructive proposal) be operationalized in a way that does not appropriate Indigenous knowledge but amplifies community-controlled narratives?
