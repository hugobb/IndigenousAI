# Technology in Language Revitalisation: Identity, Legitimacy, and Place

**Authors:** Marcela I. Huilcán Herrera
**Year:** 2022
**Venue:** Journal of Global Indigeneity, Vol. 6, No. 1

---

## Core Argument

Technologies used in Indigenous language revitalization projects do more than support linguistic goals — they actively shape Indigenous identity. Through a literature review, Herrera (a Mapuche scholar at Macquarie University and University of Groningen) argues that how technology is integrated into revitalization efforts can reinforce or undermine Indigenous identity, and that community-driven, place-aware technology use has the potential to make language reclamation a genuine act of political self-determination rather than a technical exercise.

## Key Concepts

- **Identity as sociocultural and non-static:** Drawing on Bucholtz and Hall, identity is understood as a relational, interactional construction — not a fixed psychological state. Indigenous identity is shaped by social practice, language use, and cultural participation; it is not simply a demographic category.
- **Indexicality:** Speaking an Indigenous language is an indexical act — it evokes and constructs particular identities and political stances. Language revitalization efforts are sites where Indigenous identity is continuously renegotiated and performed.
- **Language-land connection:** For Australian Indigenous peoples and others, language is ontologically tied to territory — words, names, and speech acts are co-constitutive with land. Revitalization separated from place risks producing a culturally diminished form of the language.
- **Technology as digital presence:** Inclusion of Indigenous languages in digital domains (apps, websites, social media) generates prestige and pride — evidence that the language is "good enough" for the modern world. This digital visibility is itself a form of identity assertion.
- **Legitimacy as political struggle:** Who has authority to produce language resources — linguists, technologists, or the community? This is contested terrain; digital platforms can either democratize language production or vest authority in outside experts.
- **Language reclamation = identity reclamation:** Revitalization as decolonial political act: reclaiming the language is simultaneously reclaiming cultural identity and self-determination.

## Main Findings

- The literature consistently links technology-supported language revitalization to increased feelings of pride and renewed cultural identity among community members, particularly young people.
- The most successful projects are those with the widest community participation, where community members (not outside experts) produce the digital content.
- Immersion programs and technology-supported revitalization both generate identity effects, but also anxiety: the cultural and linguistic responsibility assigned to participants can be experienced as a burden.
- Digital presence addresses the belief that Indigenous languages are not "modern" or functional — a belief that is itself a colonial artifact — by demonstrating that these languages can inhabit digital spaces.
- Legitimacy struggles are present in many projects: outside developers and linguists may inadvertently displace community authority over language and its resources.
- The connection between language, land, and identity is non-negotiable for many Indigenous communities; revitalization projects that ignore this connection produce a decontextualized version of the language.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

Herrera's framework for identity and technology directly applies to the IndigenousAI project. For Mohawk/Six Nations, the identity stakes of an AI language tool are high: a system that generates incorrect Mohawk, reinforces colonial-era language forms, or operates without community authority could undermine identity rather than support it. The indexicality point is important: every time a learner or community member interacts with an AI system that speaks Mohawk, they are receiving an implicit claim about what Mohawk is — that claim should be community-validated. The legitimacy question — who has authority over the AI system's Mohawk outputs — is the most critical governance question for the project. The place-language connection suggests that any AI tool should be designed to support, not substitute for, land-based and community-embedded language learning.

## Limitations & Critiques

- The paper is a literature review without primary data collection; it synthesizes existing studies rather than reporting new empirical findings.
- The cases reviewed span diverse contexts (Australia, Latin America, North America); generalizing across these contexts obscures important differences in colonial history, language vitality, and community structure.
- The identity framework (Bucholtz and Hall) is drawn from general sociolinguistics and may not fully capture specifically Indigenous conceptions of identity as relational to land, ancestors, and non-human beings.
- The paper was written in 2022; it predates large language models and does not address the specific identity implications of LLM-based language tools.

## Questions & Follow-ups

- How do Six Nations/Mohawk community members and Elders conceptualize the relationship between Kanien'kéha and Haudenosaunee territory? How should this inform design decisions for a Mohawk AI tool?
- What community governance structures have other revitalization technology projects used to maintain legitimacy — i.e., to ensure that community authority over the language is preserved rather than transferred to outside developers?
- Related work: Brinklow (2021) on anti-colonial ILT; Meighan (2021) on relational technology; Brinklow et al. (2019) on language reclamation; Ajani et al. (2024) on community agency and ethical consent; Godwin-Jones (2025) on place-language connection in pedagogy.
