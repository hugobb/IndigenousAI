# Data Mining and Extraction: the Gold Rush of AI on Indigenous Languages

**Authors:** Marie-Odile Junker
**Year:** 2024
**Venue:** Proceedings of the Seventh Workshop on the Use of Computational Methods in the Study of Endangered Languages (ComputEL-7), ACL 2024

---

## Core Argument

The rise of generative AI has triggered a "gold rush" on Indigenous language data, with AI developers increasingly targeting the carefully built digital resources of long-standing Indigenous language documentation projects. Drawing on three real events of data mining targeting the Algonquian Dictionaries and Language Resources project, Junker argues for an urgent code of conduct for AI development involving Indigenous languages — one grounded in OCAP® principles (Ownership, Control, Access, Possession) and community consent.

## Key Concepts

- **Data mining as extraction:** AI systems need training data; Indigenous language resources (dictionaries, conjugation tools, linguistic atlases built over decades of community collaboration) have become targets for AI companies and researchers seeking rare linguistic data.
- **The Algonquian Dictionaries project:** A 20-year collaborative infrastructure of 12 dictionaries, linguistic atlases, verb conjugators, and audio corpora for Algonquian languages (serving 20 languages, 47 dialects). Over 1 million words searched annually. Data hosted on protected Canadian servers, with tiered access by language and community.
- **OCAP® principles:** Ownership, Control, Access and Possession — the First Nations Information Governance Centre's framework for Indigenous data governance. Who owns the language data? Who controls how it is used? Who has access? Who can authorize sharing?
- **Three events (anonymized):** "The Good" — data mining with permission (European university, genuine collaboration request, agreed-upon terms); "The Bad/Ugly" — unauthorized or ethically problematic data mining events where the community's consent and sovereignty were not respected.
- **Colonialism repeat:** The extraction of natural resources (forests, rivers, soil) from Indigenous territories is now being replicated with language data — an intangible but no less important resource.

## Main Findings

- Indigenous language data is rare, inconsistently standardized (dialectal variation, orthographic inconsistency, noisy data), and difficult to use for AI — but this does not stop AI developers from attempting to extract it.
- The Algonquian Dictionaries project has had to halt some open-access and API features in response to unauthorized extraction attempts.
- Most people — including members of the communities whose languages are involved — have no idea what is happening with their language data in the AI ecosystem.
- Community members need guidance on key questions before engaging with any external AI request: Who does this data belong to? Do I have the right to give it away? Who has this right? What is the requester's true goal?
- A code of conduct is urgently needed; existing frameworks like OCAP® and CARE principles provide foundations, but adoption by the AI research community is minimal.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

This paper is a direct warning for the IndigenousAI project and any organization working with Mohawk/Kanien'kéha data. The same dynamics Junker describes — AI developers approaching Indigenous language projects as data sources, communities not understanding what they are consenting to, infrastructure being extracted for purposes the community never authorized — are live risks for the Six Nations partnership. The OCAP® framework should be foundational for any data governance agreement with the Six Nations/Mohawk community. The questions Junker poses for communities ("Who does this data belong to? What is the requester's true goal?") should be explicitly addressed in the project's community consent protocol. The warning about unauthorized API access should inform how any Mohawk language tools are built and deployed: community-controlled access, not open APIs.

## Limitations & Critiques

- The three events are anonymized; without details it is difficult to assess how representative they are or what specific harms occurred.
- The paper is a short position piece (6 pages); it identifies the problem and calls for a code of conduct but does not specify what that code should say.
- The focus is on the Algonquian language family and Canadian context; different governance frameworks apply in different jurisdictions.

## Questions & Follow-ups

- What would a workable code of conduct for AI development with Indigenous languages look like? Are there existing proposals from FNIGC, the First Peoples' Cultural Council, or other organizations?
- How should the IndigenousAI project structure its data governance agreements with Six Nations to implement OCAP® in practice?
- Related work: Brinklow (2021) on digital colonization; Brinklow et al. (2019) on data sovereignty; Kuhn et al. (2020) on NRC Canada's approach to community ownership; Dennison et al. (2026) on institutional considerations in culturally aligned AI.
