# Indigenous Language Technologies & Language Reclamation in Canada

**Authors:** Nathan Thanyehténhas Brinklow, Patrick Littell, Delaney Lothian, Aidan Pine, Heather Souter
**Year:** 2019
**Venue:** LREC 2019 Workshop on Computationally Assisted Language Documentation and Description (or associated NRC conference proceedings)

---

## Core Argument

Technology for Indigenous language revitalization in Canada shows genuine promise, but its effectiveness depends on understanding historical context, aligning with community goals, and treating process as equally important as product. The paper distinguishes "language revitalization" (restoring linguistic competence) from "language reclamation" (a broader political and cultural project of community self-determination), arguing that responsible technology development must serve the latter.

## Key Concepts

- **Language reclamation vs. revitalization:** Reclamation encompasses revitalization but is broader — it includes identity, community building, cultural resurgence, and decolonial self-determination. Technology that serves only linguistic goals while undermining community sovereignty falls short of reclamation's ambitions.
- **Technology as force multiplier:** A reframing of technology from a tool-oriented to a goal-oriented definition: technology multiplies the effectiveness of people who are doing the actual work of language reclamation; it is "the icing, not the cake."
- **Technology as MacGuffin:** A critique of the tendency to oversell technological solutions — apps and tools are frequently promoted as "saving" a language, diverting resources and energy from more effective community-centered approaches.
- **Process over product:** The development process (who is involved, how decisions are made, who owns the data) is as important as the final tool; a technology that promotes language use while separating the community from its data undermines reclamation even if it technically succeeds.
- **Rule-based approaches:** Most existing Canadian Indigenous language technologies (spell-checkers, keyboards, predictive text) rely on rule-based rather than data-driven methods due to severe data scarcity; approximately 57% of Canada's ~70 Indigenous languages have fewer than 500 active speakers.

## Main Findings

- Canada is home to approximately 70 unique Indigenous languages across 10 language families; ~57% have fewer than 500 active speakers. The decline is directly attributable to residential schools and colonial policy, with residential schools only fully closed in 1996.
- Digital technology for Indigenous languages in Canada is nascent and primarily rule-based; groups active in this space include the National Research Council of Canada, University of Alberta, First Peoples' Cultural Council, and Computer Research Institute of Montréal.
- Nearly all Canadian Indigenous languages are characterized by high morphological complexity and polysynthesis, making them substantially different from the languages for which most NLP tools were developed.
- The choice to revitalize or reclaim an Indigenous language is a political act of anticolonial resistance; technology development must be understood within this political context, not merely as a technical problem.
- Key guiding principles proposed: involve community members from problem identification through deployment; prioritize community goals over technical elegance; ensure the community retains ownership of its language data.

## Relevance to Indigenous AI

This paper, co-authored by Patrick Littell (NRC Canada) and Aidan Pine (NRC Canada) alongside Mohawk community member Nathan Brinklow, directly addresses the Canadian context of the IndigenousAI project. The principle that process is as important as product — and that data sovereignty is non-negotiable — is foundational for how the project should be structured with the Mohawk/Six Nations community. The "technology as MacGuffin" critique is a useful corrective to enthusiasm for AI-based solutions: the question is not "can we build a Mohawk LLM?" but "does this technology actually serve what the community is trying to achieve?" The rule-based vs. data-driven tension is also directly relevant: for Mohawk at current resource levels, hybrid approaches may be more appropriate than pure data-driven LLM fine-tuning.

## Limitations & Critiques

- The paper is a short (5 pages), position-oriented piece focused on principles; it does not evaluate specific technologies or present empirical findings.
- Written in 2019, it predates large language models and does not address the specific risks and opportunities that foundation models introduce for Indigenous language work.
- The principle of "process over product" is stated but not operationalized; what community governance structures, consent protocols, and data stewardship arrangements would actually implement this in practice is left underspecified.
